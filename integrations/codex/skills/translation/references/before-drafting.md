# Before drafting: purpose, source reading, strategy

Read this when the job is more than a few sentences, when no purpose was given, or when the
source is odd, old, damaged, argumentative or second-hand.

## Contents

1. Purpose: what to settle and what to assume
2. Where the source comes from
3. Reading traps
4. Oddities and defects in the source
5. Strategy decisions to fix before the first sentence

## 1. Purpose

**No brief is not a brief to stay close to the source.** "Translate this" leaves the purpose
open; it does not ask for maximum formal closeness. Infer who will read the text, where, and
what they will do with it from whatever you have (the document itself, the conversation, the
file name), state that in one or two lines, and translate for it. If the source cannot serve
the purpose you inferred, say so; do not deliver a text that serves neither.

**Settle only what changes the output.** Ask a question only if the answer would change the
translation and cannot be inferred; otherwise state the assumption and proceed. The items that
most often change it:

- the product: full translation, summary, gist, adaptation for a new audience. Omissions are
  errors only against the product that was asked for.
- the readers' knowledge of the subject. It sets terminology, how much is spelled out, and
  redundancy: lay readers need key terms explained at first mention and repeated; for
  specialists the same explicitness signals that the text is not meant for them.
- how the text is used: read once or consulted in sections (then each section must stand
  alone), read aloud, read on a sign or a phone, relied on for hours or for years.
- status, for legal and official texts: an authoritative version, a certified one, or one for
  information only. See `domains/legal.md`.
- language variant and locale, form of address, and any glossary, earlier translation or
  house style you must follow.

**Set the quality level from the use, then keep to it.** Gist, information and publication
are different products. A polished literary rendering of something wanted fast for its content
is a defect, not a bonus; so is rough wording in a text that will be published. Effort on
nuance belongs where decisions, money, health or legal consequences depend on the text.

**Accuracy is always presupposed.** A brief that lists only tone, readability or layout has not
waived fidelity. Nothing in it permits adding, dropping or altering content for fluency.

**A text written in order to be translated has only one audience.** Export advertising,
tourist information and much business correspondence never had source-culture readers. Do not
try to reproduce an effect on readers who never existed; orient every choice to the target
readers.

**Words that depend on what is not in the file.** If the text refers to images, a layout, a
form, a user interface or a table you were not given, say so and mark the renderings that
depend on them (captions, labels, "see below", button names).

## 2. Where the source comes from

Establish these before treating any feature as the author's choice.

- **Is the source itself a translation?** Signs: translationese, unidiomatic collocations,
  terminology from a third language, a translator's note, inconsistent register. A relay text
  carries the first translator's errors as if they were content. Flag it, do not reproduce
  its defects as style, and prefer the original where one exists.
- **Was it written in a second language?** Much English reaching translators is lingua-franca
  English. Odd collocations, missing idiom and flat register are then artefacts, not style:
  translate the intended meaning, and flag what is really unclear instead of reproducing or
  embellishing it.
- **Is the named author the writer?** Speeches, corporate and institutional texts are often
  written by someone else. Do not build an interpretation on stylistic quirks of a text whose
  signatory did not draft it.
- **Has it been through OCR, extraction or conversion?** Then split words, merged lines,
  running headers inside sentences and dropped diacritics are transmission damage (section 4).
- **Is it a composite?** A report containing a poem, a brochure containing recipes, a novel
  containing a contract: decide function and treatment for each embedded text separately.

## 3. Reading traps

**A sense that fits the sentence is only a hypothesis.** Before settling a polysemous word,
test the candidate against the wider text, including what comes later. If it implies
something the rest of the passage contradicts, it is wrong even when it is the word's most
frequent sense. Useful places to look: a later passage that restates the point (the writer's
own gloss), and for an abstract or evaluative word, the list of concrete items the text
itself supplies.

**The dangerous idioms are the ones that make literal sense.** An opaque idiom announces
itself. Suspect a hidden one when a literal reading is grammatical and fits the setting but is
oddly specific, banal or beside the point. In a passage about lorry drivers at a motorway
stop, "drain the radiator" reads perfectly and means something else. Confirm by checking
whether the phrase keeps company that its individual words never keep.

**Strong context makes you read what you expect.** Inside a hostile passage a clause may
concede something positive; a list of praise may contain one ironic item. Where the
surrounding text sets a strong expectation, check polarity and negation word by word against
what is written.

**Imported frames.** A misreading is rarely carelessness. It usually comes from attributing
to the text your own period's, culture's or profession's assumptions. For older or distant
texts, reconstruct the situation the text assumes before deciding what a general statement
means and whom it addresses.

**Underspecified is not the same as unclear.** When the source does not carry enough
information to fix a meaning, keep the translation as open as the source and flag it. Do not
fill the gap with the most plausible story. A confident, specific rendering of an
underspecified source is an invention, however well it reads. (Example: "Daley entering his
2nd year" does not say that Daley is two years old.) Where target grammar forces a choice the
source does not make (gender, number, formality, tense), see `sentences-and-text.md`.

**Argument has architecture.** In argumentative texts, mark the moves before translating: a
thesis cited in order to be demolished, the opposition, the grounds, the conclusion. An
implausibly extreme opening is usually someone else's view about to be attacked. A turn that
concedes and then rebuts must come out as concession plus rebuttal; if it comes out as a
neutral report, the argument is lost although every proposition survived. A parenthesis can be
a full move (a proviso, a pre-emptive defence), so test whether the argument still stands
before subordinating or trimming it. The premise linking grounds to claim is usually unstated:
reconstruct it and check that the target still supports the same inference.

**A wrong-but-grammatical word is a symptom.** When a rendering is fluent but does not fit,
or follows source word order closely, back-translate it to find which source word produced it
and re-read the source passage for a distinction you missed. Then check the sentences that
follow: one misread distinction usually reappears.

## 4. Oddities and defects in the source

Classify before acting. The three classes need opposite treatment.

1. **Transmission damage**: a word split by a line break, a page header inside a sentence,
   an obvious scanning error with exactly one possible reading. Translate the restored text.
   Mention it if it was frequent or if any case was uncertain.
2. **Possibly intentional data**: unknown acronyms, unusual spellings of names, doubled words,
   codes, odd figures, non-standard terms. Never "correct" these. Translate what is there and
   report the suspicion separately. Assume the words are intentional; the instinct to
   normalise them is the error.
3. **Defects of content or expression**: a wrong date, an inconsistent figure, an ungrammatical
   or incoherent sentence. Do not repair silently and do not reproduce blindly: see
   `intervention.md` for the decision. Report source defects separately from your own doubts.

Whether to keep an oddity of expression depends on the text. In a marked or literary text it
may be the author's and stays. In an informative text, translate the most plausible meaning
and let the oddity go. In unedited media (news sites, social posts, transcripts) stray
punctuation and broken agreement are usually accidents, while irony and colloquial
interruption are not.

**Diagnose an incoherent sentence from the error itself.** Ask which word or construction in
the writer's own language would produce exactly this mistake, and whether that word's ordinary
meaning gives a sensible reading. "The committee should be subjected to the support of local
management" traces to French *assujetti* (secured, attached), which shows what was meant.

**Check that the source supplies what the target form obliges you to state.** Some target
genres and grammars require information the source omits (a subject, a date format, a
required formula, a legally mandatory element). If the target needs more than the source and
your reliable knowledge provide, stop and obtain it or say what is missing. Never fill the gap
by invention.

## 5. Strategy decisions to fix before the first sentence

**Fix the recurrent choices in writing.** Form of address and pronoun system, level of
formality, how organisation names, titles and quotations are handled, units and date formats,
spelling variant, and the key terms. A wrong global choice produces a family of errors spread
through the text, each needing individual judgement; it cannot be repaired by search and
replace. "Press the green button", "the user presses", "the button must be pressed" and
"let's press" are four different tones.

**Decide whether the translation should read as a translation.** Ask: will the reader know
this is a translation, and do they care that there is an original?

- If the text is tied to its source occasion, author or culture (a speech, a historical
  document, a literary work, evidence), translate it as a text being quoted. Do not smooth,
  update, paraphrase or re-accent it, and do not fault the result for sounding foreign.
- If it must work as an original for comparable readers (instructions, commercial and
  institutional texts, most information), it may change wording and register freely, but its
  function may not change.
- In the second case the default assumption is that readers in both cultures expect the same
  thing. Adapt content, politeness or directness for cultural reasons only when you can name
  the difference in expectations; "cultural adaptation" without that is unlicensed rewriting.

**Rank the criteria.** The function of the translation outranks closeness to the source.
Among renderings that all serve the function, choose the one closest to the source. When
meaning, effect and form cannot all be kept, effect on the reader outranks content detail and
content outranks syntactic form, except where the text type reverses this (in a poem, form may
outrank content detail). Say what you sacrificed in the few places where it matters.

**Literal or free is decided per passage, not per job.** One document contains both needs.
A close, structure-preserving rendering is the low-risk baseline where it is natural in the
target; depart from it where the passage requires. At a point that is really undecidable, a
close rendering is legitimate risk reduction because it does not commit to an interpretation
the source does not force, whereas a smooth paraphrase does. Use it deliberately there and
note that you did.

**Ambiguity is handled by function.** Where the text is evidence or law, keep the ambiguity
visible and note both readings. Where it must work on the reader (fiction, advertising),
recreate an ambiguity of the same kind without interrupting the text.

**Beware the word that cannot be wrong.** Under doubt the safest-looking, most frequent
rendering is tempting. A chain of such choices produces a text with no attitude at all,
because the stance of the original lives in exactly the choices that were neutralised. Before
drafting, scan for evaluative loading: attitude words, intensifiers and their strength, hedges,
and how firmly the writer commits to each claim. Treat each as a decision, since fluent output
will not show whether it was handled. In a text that mixes reporting and evaluation, compare
the density of evaluative markers in source and target afterwards; if the balance has shifted,
the text has changed type.

**Spend effort where errors cost most.** Titles, headings, first and last sentences, numbers,
names, negations, conditions, instructions, warnings, quoted speech. For a hard, high-stakes
or creative unit, produce two or more candidates built on different approaches (restructure
the syntax, change the image or the level of generality, shift what is explicit), then pick
one and know why. Do not do this for routine sentences.

**Audit coherence at the end.** State the overall priority of the job in one sentence and
check that the solution to each hard passage follows it. Local solutions drift.

**Never copy an earlier rendering you know to be wrong** for the sake of consistency, even
from a supplied reference translation or glossary. Use the correct rendering and report the
divergence.
