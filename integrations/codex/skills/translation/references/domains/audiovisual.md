# Audiovisual texts

Subtitles, captions for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers (SDH), dubbing and voice-over
scripts, audio description, surtitles, songs. The text is one channel among several: the
viewer also has the picture, the sound and a clock. Formatting details (hyphens for two
speakers, italics, ellipses, capitals, colours, label syntax) differ between broadcasters and
platforms and contradict each other; **follow the client's style guide for those and ask if
none was supplied.** What follows is what holds across them.

## 1. Subtitles

**Space and time.** Unless the brief says otherwise: at most two lines, roughly 35 to 42
characters per line for Latin scripts (fewer for wide scripts), on screen from about one to
about six seconds, a reading speed that leaves time to look at the picture. A subtitle never
runs past a cut into another scene.

**Condensation is the job, and a model does not do it unprompted.** Asked to translate
dialogue, a model keeps hesitations, repetitions and fragments and produces something too
long to read. Plan for an overall reduction against the spoken source.

- **Reformulate; do not merely delete.** The limit is permission to rebuild the sentence.
  Spread small cuts across sentences in preference to dropping a whole clause, and delete a
  whole sentence only when it adds nothing the picture or context lacks.
- **Cut what the image already says.** Do not subtitle what the viewer can see or will
  understand anyway (a name being shouted for the tenth time, identical on-screen text).
  But where the picture depends on the repetition (someone pointing at several things in
  turn), keep it.
- **Protected material.** Names used in direct address. The first and last words of a
  stretch of speech when the speaker is visible. The words carrying the core proposition,
  which should be catchable at a glance and not buried at the end.
- **Do not buy brevity with politeness or conflict.** Under compression the first things to
  go are mitigators ("please", modals, "I wonder if") and the sharpest words. An indirect
  request reduced to a bare imperative makes a character rude; a softened quarrel makes them
  conciliatory. Audit both after condensing. Shifts that stack in one direction change how a
  whole group of characters is perceived.
- **Do not shorten by changing tense, aspect or level of formality.** Contracting formal
  speech or expanding casual speech changes the speaker.
- **Prefer short, frequent, colloquial words and connectives,** and keep reference chains
  intact; a compressed subtitle with rare words and loose pronouns is harder to read than a
  longer plain one.
- **Hesitations and interjections** are kept where they characterise the speaker or matter
  to the plot and cut where they are noise.

**Line breaks.** Break at clause and phrase boundaries: at punctuation where possible,
otherwise before a conjunction or preposition. Never separate article from noun, adjective
from noun, first name from surname, subject pronoun from verb, or the parts of a verb. Do not
rebalance line lengths at the cost of a sense unit. Never end a subtitle with a comma or
semicolon.

**Naturalness.** Subtitled and dubbed dialogue most often sounds translated in its fixed
phrases, routine formulae, interjections and fillers. Run a separate pass on those and use
the target's own, including where the source gives no direct trigger.

**Other points.**

- **A joke or line that depends on something visible** cannot be translated from the words
  alone. Get a description of the picture, or flag it. For a verbal-visual pun, start from
  the image: find a target expression whose literal sense is what is shown.
- **Never swap in a different brand, company or celebrity,** and do not replace a
  culture-bound item with the target's nearest familiar thing while the original is on
  screen.
- **Strong language is not softened.** Match severity and intent, not the dictionary
  equivalent. Where the audio itself is bleeped, show that it was.
- **Dialect.** Where a target dialect would be comic or stereotyping, keep standard grammar
  and mark the speech with colloquial vocabulary. Do not correct grammar that belongs to a
  dialect. Do not use a sociolect that signals a different social position (recent migrant
  for native minority).
- **Approved titles and on-screen titles** come from the project's records, not from a fresh
  translation.
- **Songs** are subtitled when the lyrics carry narrative or emotional weight.
- **A template or timing file from another language is a reference, not a constraint.** You
  remain responsible for segmentation, repetitions and reading speed in the target.
- **Translating into a pivot language for onward translation:** leave measurements,
  currencies, names and cultural references unlocalised, keep sentences plain, and annotate
  what would otherwise be lost (wordplay, register, relationships, who is addressed, gender
  where the pivot language hides it).

## 2. Captions for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers

- **Judge them with the sound off.** That is the audience's position.
- **Fidelity is the default.** Equal access means not withholding or simplifying. Edit only
  as far as reading speed forces.
- **Describe what is heard, not what is seen.** Label a sound only if it matters to plot or
  atmosphere or the picture does not identify it. Sudden loud sounds are labelled even when
  their source is visible. A label names the sound, with volume, speed or manner where
  relevant, not the action on screen.
- **Give the interjection itself** in preference to a label that interprets it.
- **Speaker identification must not reveal what the viewer is not yet meant to know.** An
  unnamed speaker is a neutral label until the story names them.
- **Accent or dialect** is indicated once where it matters to the story and then carried
  sparingly by vocabulary and a few respellings, never by transcribing every word
  phonetically.
- **When speech cannot be made out,** caption the objective cause, not a guess at the words.
- **Music:** make the source inferable (mood alone when the player is visible, mood plus
  source when not), and vary labels for different cues.

## 3. Dubbing and voice-over

- **Text written to be spoken gets a spoken-syntax pass.** Split long written periods and
  say each line aloud.
- **Lip synchronisation matters only where the mouth is visible,** in close and frontal
  shots, and there mainly for consonants that visibly close the lips. Elsewhere length and
  rhythm matter. Lines must also agree with gesture, head movement and facial expression:
  never achieve lip-sync by contradicting the body.
- **After fitting lines to lips, re-check register.** A word chosen because it fits often
  makes the character more formal or less consistent. Audit slang and swearing for
  downgrading and restore the force or compensate nearby.
- **Voice-over** is in the first person of the original speaker. Stretches where the
  original voice is heard alone, before or after the overlay, are rendered more closely,
  because some of the audience can compare.
- **Songs to be sung:** singability (open vowels, few consonant clusters), sense, rhythm and
  rhyme compete. Decide which one gives way according to the purpose, and expect to lose
  some meaning.

## 4. Audio description

- **Describe only in the gaps** between dialogue and significant sound. Where an important
  passage leaves no gap, describe the coming change early, in the previous pause.
- **Select by narrative relevance, not visual prominence:** who is present, what they do and
  how, cause and effect, changes of place and time, direction of gaze, and what a spoken
  "this" or "there" points to.
- **Do not reveal an identity before sighted viewers could know it.**
- **Colour is included** for its cultural and symbolic meaning.
- **Scripts read aloud by a voice** need homographs and homophones disambiguated by wording.

## 5. Surtitles and stage captions

Convey what is sung or said, not how. Meaning and rhythm before rhyme, no explanatory
additions, shorter lines than screen subtitles. For chants and heavy repetition a summary
label lets the audience watch the stage.

## 6. Evaluating audiovisual translation

First align picture and text to establish what the viewer gets from both. Condensation and
omission that fit the space and are covered by the image are not errors; losses of force,
politeness, plot information or a speaker's character are. Judge dialogue by whether the
speaker's intent reaches the audience, not only by propositional accuracy.
