# Legal and official texts

Legislation, treaties, contracts, judgments, certificates, testimony and anything that will be
relied on in a legal setting. The general rules of this skill bend here in specific ways:
variation is an error, clarity can be a falsification, and natural target wording can change
someone's rights. Read `intervention.md` first for the general rules on errors and ambiguity;
this file states where legal work differs.

## 1. Four questions before the first word

1. **What status will the translation have?** An authentic version that will itself be law
   or contract; a certified translation for a court or authority; or a translation for
   information only. Nearly every choice below depends on this. If you cannot tell, ask; if
   you cannot ask, treat it as for information and say so.
2. **Which legal system is the system of reference?** Find the governing-law clause in a
   contract and the language clause naming the prevailing version. If the target system
   governs, its established formulae are used. If the source system governs, its formulae are
   rendered so that they still point to the source system. Decide this passage by passage
   where an instrument imports institutions from another system.
3. **Which jurisdiction and which date?** The same word names different concepts in
   jurisdictions that share a language (and in sub-national units of one state), and a term
   in an old document means what it meant there and then, not what its cognate means now.
4. **Who will interpret the text, and how?** Courts that give effect to every word need
   circumstances spelled out and tolerate no additions. Courts that read by purpose tolerate
   more. Set literalness from that, not from habit.

Expect difficulty to follow the distance between the two legal systems more than the distance
between the two languages.

## 2. The standard: legal effect

- **Test each rendering by what a court or an opposing lawyer would do with it:** who may
  act, by what procedure, what each party gains or loses. Dictionary closeness is not the
  test.
- **When they conflict:** the intent of the instrument first, the legal effect second,
  literal sameness of wording last. A rendering that is more exact or more fluent but has a
  different effect loses.
- **Literalism is not the safe option.** Clinging to source wording out of caution produces
  target terms that mean something else in the target system.
- **Legal texts direct; they do not describe.** Obligation, prohibition, permission and
  authorisation must come out as such. Split a document by function: parts that regulate
  future conduct, parts that report, parts that argue. Each takes different handling.

## 3. Terms across legal systems

- **Ordinary-looking words are the suspects.** Before rendering a common word, establish
  whether it has a technical legal sense here, in which branch of law (contract, property,
  family, insurance), and verify it against the institution in the source system.
- **Find the equivalent by solving the problem, not by looking up the word.** State the
  situation the source term deals with and its outcome, ask how the target system handles
  that situation, and take that institution's name as the candidate.
- **Then test the candidate on three points:** its branch of law and place in the hierarchy
  of concepts; its scope (list the proceedings, transactions or persons each term actually
  covers); its legal effects. For terms that define an offence or liability, any difference
  in scope changes who is caught.
- **The type of equivalent tells the reader which system to read the term by.** A borrowing
  or literal rendering points to the source system; a natural target term invites the target
  system's concept with everything attached to it. Choose deliberately.
- **A near equivalent with the wrong scope is not used bare.** Add a delimiting qualifier, or
  keep the original term with a short gloss in brackets. A narrower term may render a broader
  one only where the narrow scope suffices; never the reverse.
- **For a court, authority or office with no counterpart, never substitute the target body
  whose name looks closest.** Keep the designation with a gloss, or describe it neutrally.
- **Never collapse two source terms onto one target word** because the target system lacks
  the distinction. Never delete a term that carries legal status for want of an equivalent.
- **Existing official usage binds.** Before choosing a term in anything with the force of
  law, search the instruments already in force in that system and field and adopt their
  equivalent, even if you think it inferior. Names of courts, ministries, agencies,
  conventions and treaties are looked up, not translated. Standardised terms (trade terms,
  standards vocabulary, statutory definitions) come from the issuing body's official text.
- **International and supranational instruments avoid national terms of art.** Use neutral
  wording, and where a text creates concepts of its own, a fresh designation in each
  language. Latin is not neutral: the same Latin expression has system-specific senses.
- **Legal terminology, not a lay description,** in an instrument. A notice that informs lay
  people of their rights is the reverse case: write so that they understand, and keep the
  legal term beside the explanation.
- **Regulated wording.** For compliance notices, statutory warnings and regulated product
  terms, check whether the target market prescribes the exact text.

## 4. The wording of provisions

- **No variation, ever.** The same source term or formula gets the same target expression
  every time; within an instrument a different word is read as a different meaning. Where two
  renderings are equally good, pick one and keep it. Check both directions: source terms
  with more than one target term, and target terms standing for more than one source term.
  The second list finds more.
- **Where the source itself uses two near-synonyms for one referent,** do not assume the
  variation is meaningful, and report the question.
- **Defined terms get the strictest fidelity in the document.** Do not paraphrase or
  restructure a definition. Keep its genus, every listed component and every exclusion, and
  its scope formula ("for the purposes of this Part"). "Means" makes a definition exhaustive
  and "includes" leaves the ordinary meaning in place: they never share a target verb, though
  check first what a given "includes" is actually doing.
- **Every word is operative.** Add nothing explanatory or emphatic. Before dropping any word,
  ask what it does to the class of persons, things, conditions or time limits covered.
- **Doublets and word strings** ("null and void", "terms, covenants and conditions") are not
  collapsed by default. Test whether the members can be distinguished in law.
- **Keep the level of specification item by item.** Every enumerated item, catch-all phrase
  and qualification stays. Do not turn a list into a general principle or a principle into a
  list: across the common-law and civil-law divide that changes what the provision covers.
- **Modals are legal acts.** Map each to its force: conferring a power or permission,
  imposing a duty, creating a condition, declaring a legal effect. Render that force with the
  form the target system's drafting uses for it (which may be a present indicative), not with
  the grammatical counterpart. Decide for each duty whether breach invalidates the act or is
  a mere irregularity, and keep that strength.
- **A limiting rule is not a permission.** "May be refused only if X" gives the court a
  choice. The continental construction makes refusal conditional on X and says nothing about
  choice. Check every "only" and every modal that can mean permission or possibility.
- **Check every "and" and "or" in a rule's conditions.** These errors pass revision
  unnoticed. Where the source connector is ambiguous, do not choose.
- **Scope of modifiers and stacked conditions.** Decide which elements each qualifies in the
  source and make the target attach it to the same ones. Where the source is ambiguous about
  that, reword so that the target is ambiguous in the same way; do not let target grammar
  settle it.
- **Negation and proof.** Before turning a negative provision positive or the reverse, find
  the element a party would have to prove. If the change moves the negation into or out of
  that element, keep the original form. Negative definitions built on exclusions are left
  exactly as they are.
- **"Subject to" and "provided that" set precedence,** not a loose connection.
- **Pronouns.** Check that the target's gender and number still pick out the same antecedent.
- **Punctuation can be part of the enacted text** and is construed by courts. Do not treat it
  as cosmetic.
- **Final proof of the small words** against the source: any, other, all, every, only, not,
  unless, the modals, definite against indefinite article, "or" against "and", time limits.

## 5. Ambiguity, vagueness and defects

- **Vagueness in a negotiated instrument is part of the deal.** In treaties, conventions,
  resolutions and many contracts, reproduce the same degree of openness and report it.
  Imprecision may signal that an undertaking is political rather than binding; a clearer
  translation turns it into something else. Only a court may settle what was left open.
- **Is an obscure passage a compromise or a mistake?** Check the drafting record and the
  other language versions. If you cannot tell, treat it as deliberate.
- **Evident errors and wrong identifying data** (names, addresses, account and registry
  numbers) are carried into the target as they stand and reported separately. Do not repair
  them in the text.
- **Before concluding that a passage is pointless or unintelligible,** look for the genre
  convention that explains it: why the clause sits there, whom it addresses.
- **A consequential term with two readings that lead to different actions** is never settled
  by which reading fits the sentence better. Establish the intended one or flag both.
- **Witnesses and lay documents.** Where a statement is vague or uses a culture-specific
  expression, render it as it stands. Resolving it into a specific fact, time or institution
  creates evidence.

## 6. Sentence structure and layout

- **Map the logical skeleton first:** main clause, conditions, exceptions, provisos, and what
  each attaches to. Build the target from the map. For a rule of the "if P then Q" kind,
  separate the case and its conditions from the legal subject and the legal action.
- **Whether you may split a sentence depends on the status.** Where versions are cited in
  parallel or will be equally authentic, keep sentence boundaries, numbering and the division
  into paragraphs identical; later references depend on them. In a translation for
  information, splitting is allowed if every condition stays attached to the right element.
  A one-sentence section in a common-law statute is a convention, and splitting it produces
  repetition or lost links.
- **Order.** Elements of the fact-situation may be reordered. Elements of the statement of
  law are not, without a specific reason; in criminal provisions the conduct precedes the
  penalty. Alternatives joined by "or" are never merged or redistributed.
- **Keep the parts of an instrument:** the long title in full (it may be enacted and used in
  interpretation), the enacting formula as words that make law and not as a report of
  enactment, recitals, final clauses. The designation an instrument bears (treaty,
  convention, protocol, charter) is kept, not normalised.
- **In a translation of legislation for information, keep the source's own names for its
  divisions** (a German § stays §) so that citations can be followed.
- **Use target-language parallel documents to learn terminology and phrasing, not layout.**
  Importing the target genre's structure breaks correspondence with the source.

## 7. Several language versions

- **Read the other authentic versions before deciding what an unclear term means,** and align
  your rendering with all of them.
- **No version is the original.** An equally authentic text is drafted to stand alone as law
  with the same effects, not as a reproduction of another version.
- **Do not make your version broader as a margin of safety.** Where authentic texts differ in
  breadth, the common meaning prevails, so the surplus achieves nothing and creates a
  divergence.
- **Do not silently resolve an ambiguity in one version.** Reproduce it so that the versions
  can still be reconciled, and raise it.
- **Verify parallelism mechanically:** numbering, cross-references, headings, the position of
  each passage. Definition sections may legitimately be ordered differently in each language.
- **A quotation of, or amendment to, an instrument that already exists in the target
  language uses the published wording verbatim.**
- **A proposed change goes into every version at once.**

## 8. By genre

- **Contracts.** Formulae follow the use the translation will serve: the target system's own
  where the contract will operate there, a rendering that tracks the source where it serves
  to understand a contract governed elsewhere.
- **Treaties and diplomatic texts.** Look up official titles. The party in whose language the
  text is written is conventionally named first. A document meant not to create obligations
  (memorandum of understanding, joint communiqué) avoids the vocabulary of agreement and
  obligation; do not introduce it.
- **Judgments.** Not uniformly literal: the operative part (ruling, order) stays closest,
  the narrative of facts has more room. Keep the parties' arguments formally distinct from
  the court's own statements; each language marks reported argument differently. Verify which
  argument the court accepts. Do not add reasoning to a terse judgment or compress a
  discursive one, and do not smooth awkward phrasing that may be carrying the reasoning. In a
  judgment written as one long sentence, read the operative decision at the end first.
- **Testimony, interviews and records of speech.** Render completely: no condensation or
  polish, register kept, hesitations and repetitions kept where the record is evidence. Keep
  each speaker in the first person. In questioning, the form of a question is its force:
  keep its degree of control, its tag questions and discourse markers, and add no
  politeness. Do not raise a lay writer's letter into legal register.
- **Notices of rights and procedural information for lay readers.** Translate for
  comprehension, not for formal correspondence with the source.
- **Requests to simplify.** For specialists: restructure syntax, keep genre and terms. For lay
  readers: a different, explanatory document, declared as such. In both, dropping a
  qualifier or condition is a change of rights, not simplification.

## 9. Delivery

- **Mark the document as a translation,** naming the source language, and mark its end.
- **Keep queries, caveats and comments out of the translated instrument.** They go in a
  covering note. A short bracketed gloss after a retained foreign term is part of the
  translation; commentary is not.
- **Translation for information only:** state that only the source is valid.
- **State the limit.** A document destined for a court or authority usually needs a
  translator authorised in that jurisdiction; neither machine output nor an ordinary
  translation should be presented as valid for that purpose. Making a foreign instrument
  "valid and binding" under the target law is legal drafting, a separate task needing
  qualified review. Say so instead of doing it silently.
