# Literary texts

Fiction, poetry, drama, essays and memoir: texts whose manner is part of what they say. The
default instincts of a fluent writer (clarify, smooth, vary, tidy, explain) are the main
source of damage here. Most of this file is a list of things not to improve.

## 1. Before drafting

- **Place the piece in the whole.** For an excerpt, chapter or poem, establish where it sits
  in the work and what the author's characteristic senses of key words are across the work.
- **State the subtext to yourself:** what the surface narrative implies and does not say.
  Word choices are then made for their connotation in its service.
- **When one stylistic feature characterises the whole text** (pervasive dryness, eccentric
  rhythm, metaphorical density), plan for it globally. Solving it feature by feature loses
  it.
- **Map foreground and background.** Note what the source puts in main clauses and completed
  tenses and what in subordinate or ongoing ones. The target must keep the same events in
  front.
- **In narration mixed with speech and thought,** separate the layers of a sentence first
  (the narrative statement, then whose words or thoughts each remaining part is).
- **A bare line of dialogue is translated from the preceding text.** Speaker, gender,
  addressee, tense and pronoun reference are usually fixed several lines earlier. If a
  passage keeps producing the wrong subject, widen the context by several paragraphs instead
  of rewriting the sentence.
- **Declare the product.** Translation, adaptation (cuts, interpolations, changed period or
  style) and free variation are different deliverables. Name which this is.

## 2. What not to improve

- **Vagueness.** Where a source word is underspecified, take the least specific target
  option, not a detail you can infer. Do not expand a generic epithet.
- **Implicitness.** Do not gloss culture-bound details in passing, replace a terse statement
  with a descriptive verb, or add nouns naming what the scene implies. Keep exclamations as
  short as the source's.
- **Withheld information.** Where an effect depends on what the reader does not yet know
  (openings, suspense, ironic set-ups), keep the exact order in which information is released
  and do not let a title or an early line give away what the source holds back.
- **Difficulty.** Do not paraphrase a condensed passage to make it easier. Its indeterminacy
  is doing work.
- **Oddity.** Test whether a strange collocation, broken sequence or unexpected ellipsis is
  systematic. If it recurs, it is designed. An action attributed to something that cannot
  perform it, or a breach of real-world logic, is reproduced, not rationalised. A coined
  word that is uncanny in the source is rendered to stay uncanny.
- **Rhythm.** A translation can read smoothly because it has dissolved long periodic
  sentences, flattened subordination and moved emphatic elements. Sentence boundaries may
  move where the target needs it, but where length, breathlessness or plainness is a
  signature (very long sentences at a climax, bare unmarked sentences in an "artless"
  style), it is preserved. Where tempo comes from structure, count the units and reproduce
  the pattern.
- **Lists without connectives stay without connectives.** In a build-up whose last item
  explains the others, the order stays.
- **Punctuation that is scarce or clustered is marked.** Exclamation marks stay at their
  original sites; unpunctuated litanies are not given commas.
- **Position of evaluation.** Where the source fronts an evaluative word, keep it in opening
  position. Contrasted elements (here and there, then and now) keep the same grammatical
  slot.
- **Possessive or article** fixes whose perspective owns a thing ("her mission", "the
  mission"). Abstract nouns are audited as closely as adjectives: replacing one with another
  ("adventure" with "mission") reframes the story.
- **Narrative mediation.** Indirect speech filtered through the narrator is not turned into
  direct quotation. In free indirect discourse, register and vocabulary shift with the
  character whose view it is.
- **Do not add effects.** No foreshadowing, echo, rhyme or irony the source lacks, even where
  your wording makes one available. Read the target for accidental sound effects (an
  unintended rhyme, a run of alliteration, a homonym) and remove them.
- **Do not import target-culture attitudes.** A neutral source term for a group is not
  upgraded to a slur or downgraded to a current euphemism, and inclusive forms the source
  lacks are not added. Proper names are not swapped for loaded ones.
- **Source-shaped syntax is not fidelity.** None of the above licenses carrying over clause
  order and sentence architecture. Deviant target syntax is used only to mirror a deliberate
  deviation, because readers attribute it to the author.

## 3. Repetition, motifs and key words

Three different cases:

- **A device.** A word repeated conspicuously, a refrain, a fixed epithet, a framing word in
  title, first and last sentence, a motif stem recurring across distant passages, a
  philosophical keyword, the small set of marked words an author's voice rests on: one
  target form, every time. Elegant variation destroys it. Build a target set of comparable
  size and draw every occurrence from it.
- **An ordinary recurring word whose sense shifts with context and speaker:** decide it per
  occurrence. Forcing one equivalent is the terminology habit misapplied.
- **A repetition you created,** because two different source words in one sentence both map
  to the same target word: recast.

When a text runs on a motif (a semantic field such as cards or ships) and one instance cannot
be carried over, plant an instance nearby. No target text reproduces all the source's chains
of related words: decide which chains matter and protect those.

Distinct words for one referent, and pairs of near-synonyms used for intensity, stay distinct.

## 4. Voices

- **The unit of register is the character's whole way of speaking.** Infer each speaker's
  social and educational level from the entire work, commit to a matching target variety,
  and hold it. Speakers marked by class, region or profession must not converge on one
  neutral standard.
- **Dialect.** Do not replace a source regional dialect with a target regional dialect (it
  relocates the story) and do not invent a synthetic one. Signal non-standard speech
  sparingly, with a few markers of general spoken or socially comparable usage. If it cannot
  be marked in place, compensate nearby so that the overall level is kept.
- **Social relations marked by a device the target lacks** (formal and familiar pronouns,
  honorifics, name plus patronymic): substitute a target device that marks the same
  relation; do not explain it. Translate the relation a form of address encodes, not the
  form. Keep titles level: "Mrs Smith" does not become "Mum".
- **A deliberate breach of manners** (refusing a title, a demeaning form of address) needs a
  breach of comparable size in the target.
- **Swearing and insults** are rendered by emotional force, with errors guarded in both
  directions. Not every instance needs a counterpart: sometimes none is the only natural
  line, and where the source repeats one term the target may need several. Place a vulgar
  term on its axes before choosing (slang or colloquial, vulgar or accepted, rising or
  dated). Animal-based insults and endearments take the target's conventional expression.
  A female character's vulgarity is not toned down.
- **Discourse markers and involving devices** ("you know", "I mean", tags) build tone and
  complicity. They are not filler.
- **Other languages inside the text.** Keep an embedded foreign passage if the source's own
  readers had to cope with it; mark it consistently. Where the embedded language is the
  target language itself, keep it visibly marked so that otherness does not turn into
  sameness. Words borrowed from the target language that were marked in the source need
  their markedness recreated.

## 5. Allusion, wordplay and culture-bound items

- **Test an allusion by whether the target reader can recognise it,** not by whether you can
  name it. A quotation from a known work uses the established target version. A text
  originally written in the target language is quoted in its original wording, never
  translated back.
- **A concealed allusion stays concealed.** At most plant a clue of the same kind.
- **Where an allusion's job is to characterise** (marks a speaker as well read) and the work
  means nothing to target readers, another recognised reference doing the same job may
  replace it, provided it does not clash with the setting. A name used as a byword takes the
  target's byword or the trait itself.
- **Wordplay.** Identify which element generates the joke, replace that element, and rebuild
  around it, in the same turn and position where possible. Where a passage turns on how a
  word is spelled or sounds, find the corresponding play in the target's own system. An
  idiom played on so that both its literal and figurative senses are live is not replaced
  by another idiom of similar meaning.
- **Is the marked form the point or an ornament?** Where a pun or alliteration merely
  decorates, it may be given up; where the passage exists for it, it must be recreated.
- **Compensation.** If an effect cannot be produced where it occurs, do not force it and do
  not explain it. Introduce an effect of the same kind nearby.
- **Culture-bound items** sort into those that must be explained, those that must not, and
  those that can be inferred. Something mentioned in passing takes a generic equivalent, not
  a gloss. A recurring term is kept and glossed once, briefly, at first occurrence. Choose
  one naming convention for the book (source-culture names, or target replacements) and
  never mix them.
- **Images are tested against target associations.** What an animal, colour or natural
  phenomenon stands for differs. Check also what motive your image attributes: a network
  rendered as a chain or an empire changes the actor.
- **Children's lore** (rhymes, games, riddles, cartoon characters) cannot be looked up
  reliably and is not translated literally. Treat it as a research problem.
- **Text in an image that belongs to the depicted place** (a street sign) is not translated.
  Do not put into words what a picture already shows.

## 6. Time and period

- **Benchmark against the source's first readers.** Language that was ordinary and current
  for them becomes ordinary current target language. Only what was archaic or marked then is
  marked now.
- **For an older text, a few still-comprehensible archaic forms are enough** to signal age.
  Do not write in a past state of the target language and do not invent a mock-antique
  style; build one consistent, slightly distanced register.
- **No current slang in a period text,** and none whose currency you cannot vouch for.
  Topical slang dates a translation within a decade.
- **Check the first attested date of conspicuous target words,** above all eponyms and
  technical terms, in a period text.
- **Test an obvious-looking word in an old source against its period sense.** If the modern
  reading gives a strange result, it has changed meaning.
- **A modern edition of a premodern text is already an interpretation.** Its punctuation and
  paragraphing were added.
- **Units, money and time-reckoning of another period or culture stay as they are** where
  context gives the scale.

## 7. Poetry and other constrained forms

- **Decide what the target audience will accept as a poem** (rhymed, blank, free) and which
  features you will carry, before drafting. Do not assume the target form with the same name
  is the equivalent; check how it moves.
- **Sense first in drafting, form in the reworking.** Start from a semantically close
  version and rework it for sound and rhythm in later passes.
- **Where a formal principle makes the effect** (rhyme, metre, parallel build-up, a proverb's
  balance), build a new form that produces the effect and let the content move. Replace the
  device, not the sound, and do not copy devices in the same positions at the cost of sense.
- **When rhyme cannot be sustained without padding,** loosen it (assonance, internal rhyme)
  before you strain the diction. In a constrained line, swap the key word for a near
  relation that satisfies the constraint.
- **Generate candidates on form first** for sound-patterned text, and judge meaning second.
- **Read every version aloud.** A rendering that sounds bad is wrong.
- **Not everything survives.** A coherent poem with a life of its own outranks exhaustive
  transfer. A prose rendering of verse is a transformation; say so.
- **Drama for performance** is constrained by breath, movement and timing as much as by the
  source. Put maximum precision where the moment turns and allow licence elsewhere.
- **Concrete and visual poetry** is rebuilt, not word-substituted.

## 8. Titles, notes and apparatus

- **Change a title only for a stated reason** (an unfamiliar name, no resonance, an
  untranslatable pun), test candidates for connotation and for whether the image can recur
  in the body, and settle it last.
- **No explanatory notes inside the body of a novel or story.** If explanation is
  unavoidable, use a glossary or endnotes. Inventive solutions can be documented there.
- **Place an in-text addition where the surrounding lines still work,** and check the next
  line: a reply may presuppose that the speaker did not explain.
- **Before leaving anything out, check what later text depends on it.** Replies and pronouns
  attach to the deleted content. Never solve a difficulty by deleting; what may be omitted
  is an explanation, not the passage.
- **Working drafts carry flags and queries; the delivered text carries none.** Remove or
  convert every bracketed marker.
- **Working from a translation and not the original:** say so in the delivered text.
- **Structural changes** (reordered sections, cut chapters, a selection from a long work)
  are declared. For children: fix the age band, keep the original's voice and difficulty,
  and treat any removal of harsh material as an adaptation to be agreed and declared, not a
  default.

## 9. Errors, oddities and uncertain sources

- **An absurd image in your draft, or a mismatch with the dictionary entry** (wrong gender,
  unexpected capital), is evidence that you misread. Stop and re-read the source.
- **A literal reply that nobody would say in that situation** is probably a fixed expression.
  So is a phrase that looks oddly specific or ungrammatical: test it as an idiom or allusion.
- **A word no dictionary has, resembling a near neighbour, is more likely a misprint** than
  rare slang. Words that do not exist, broken syntax and contradictions that persist under
  careful reading are evidence of a defective source, not of your failing.
- **Before correcting a factual error:** could the author have corrected it and did not; does
  the text suggest it is deliberate; is it a character's error and not the author's? In a
  form-focused text the author's errors stay and are reported. An obvious misprint is
  corrected and recorded.
- **An undecipherable word is not given a plausible meaning.** Report it.
- **An uncertain source** (variant readings, damaged passages, no definitive edition): choose
  the reading that coheres best and record which edition you followed. Gaps and blank lines
  may be meaningful (censored or withheld material). Do not close them silently.
- **An inconsistent grammatical feature** (historical present in some passages only): check
  whether the inconsistency is meaningful before regularising.
- **For concrete physical things** (garments, tools, parts of objects), settle doubt with a
  picture, not a definition.
- **Comparing two existing translations of a hard passage** shows where they diverge, and
  the divergences mark the places where the source is doing something deliberate. Use this
  after your own draft, not before.
