# Medical and pharmaceutical texts

Patient information, consent forms, labels and leaflets, clinical and regulatory documents,
research papers, and healthcare conversations. What is specific here: many items have an
official answer per market, small words carry clinical risk, and the same content is written
quite differently for patients and for professionals.

## 1. Items that are looked up, never translated

- **Trade names.** Do not carry a medicine's brand name over from the source. Find the name
  the same product is sold under in the target market; one active substance has different
  brands in different countries. If you cannot verify one, use the substance name and say so.
- **Substance names.** An approved name is not necessarily international. The same substance
  can have separate official names under the international, US, British and Japanese
  systems. Use the one valid for the target market.
- **Prescribed headings and formulae.** In regulated genres (summaries of product
  characteristics, patient leaflets, labels, trial protocols, consent forms) the section
  headings and many standard sentences (warnings, contraindications, pregnancy and storage
  statements) have an official target wording. Take it from the regulator's published
  templates; do not translate afresh.
- **Safety-reporting vocabulary.** Adverse event, adverse drug reaction, serious adverse
  event and serious adverse drug reaction are four distinct regulatory concepts. "Serious" is
  not "severe".
- **Eponyms.** A condition may carry several competing eponyms, and medical cultures have
  settled on different ones. Check which the target uses.
- **Acronyms.** Never expand one from its letters or its most familiar sense; the same
  letters stand for unrelated concepts. Resolve it from this text and verify.
- **Who may receive the text.** Before translating promotional material for a medicine,
  establish who may legally be addressed in the target market. Advertising a
  prescription-only product to the public is prohibited in many jurisdictions, and that
  changes the job.

## 2. Small words with clinical weight

- **Hedging in risk and consent text is kept at exactly its strength:** "may", "sometimes",
  "in some people", "rarely". Not strengthened into assertions and not softened for flow.
- **Causal strength is a scale.** "Causes", "is associated with", "has been implicated in",
  "may result in" and "is thought to cause" are different claims. Rank the source's causal
  expressions and keep each at its rank.
- **Near-synonyms are distinct concepts** and are never alternated for variety: dose and
  dosage, incidence and prevalence, mortality and lethality, accuracy and precision, trial
  and study, infectious and contagious.
- **Look-alike words:** check the concept, not the resemblance. Clinical English "evidence",
  "fatal", "disorder", "labour" and "drug" each have traps in several target languages.
- **Etymology suggests a meaning; it does not fix it.** One root covers several referents
  (bone marrow, spinal cord and myelin share one; bladder and sac share another).
- **Body-part words divide the body differently** between languages (hand and arm, foot and
  leg). Resolve from the anatomical detail in the text.
- **Dosage.** Keep each number with its unit. Where the genre's conventions allow, write
  easily misread units in full and avoid decimals by using a smaller unit. Never recalculate
  a dose on your own initiative.
- **Ambiguity in instructions is removed, not preserved.** Where a step admits two readings,
  choose the one that makes the instruction executable and flag the assumption, or ask.

## 3. Readership decides the register, both ways

- **For lay readers, no clinical term is left bare.** Plain wording first with the term
  after, or the term glossed in brackets. Expect the text to grow: simplification adds
  explanation. Match the everyday word where the source word is everyday, pair by pair.
- **For specialists, keep the field's current jargon,** including established borrowings and
  calques that a prescriptive dictionary would reject. Removing it marks the text as not
  written for them.
- **A mixed register may be the genre.** Where the source moves between technical,
  semi-technical and plain terms for one thing and the target genre does the same, keep the
  mixture.
- **A term proposed by a clinician:** trust their judgement of the concept, check their
  wording against texts written in the target language. Specialists who read mainly in
  English carry English interference.
- **Wording about patients.** Use the person-first, non-reifying forms the target language
  has established ("patient", not "case"; people with a diagnosis, not the diagnosis as a
  noun for the person). Do not mention a condition unless the sentence needs it.
- **Euphemism thresholds differ.** A word for a body part or function that is neutral
  clinical vocabulary in the source may be crude, comic or prissy in the target.
- **A misleading official label** (an institutional euphemism, an inaccurate name) is not
  quietly laundered into the standard term. Keep it and note the discrepancy, or use the
  precise term and say so.

## 4. Genre and structure

- **Read two or three target exemplars of a formulaic genre first.** What they all share is
  obligatory; the rest is optional. Follow the target genre's own section order where the
  brief allows restructuring (a patient fact sheet runs from what the condition is, through
  symptoms and treatment, to outlook).
- **A leaflet derived from product documentation is checked against that documentation** for
  the same product in the target market: indications, doses, contraindications, warnings.
- **Consent forms:** the authorisation is in the first person of the signer. The statements
  that participation is voluntary and may be withdrawn without penalty, and the signature
  block, are kept exactly.
- **Source quality.** Medical sources are often written by people who are not writers, not
  native speakers, or both. Follow `intervention.md`: plain competent prose, defects
  reported.
- **Translation for information only** (the reader knows it is a translation and needs the
  content): get the content right and stop. Polishing it to read like original writing is
  wasted effort and a risk.
- **A research finding rewritten for the general public** is a different product (result
  first, academic framing dropped). It needs to be the brief.
- **Ethical priority follows the genre:** factual validity where health depends on accuracy;
  confidentiality in patient records, consents and development files, with nothing
  identifying reproduced elsewhere; respectful, non-stigmatising wording in patient-facing
  texts.

## 5. Healthcare conversations

- **Do not triage by your own idea of medical relevance.** When relaying or summarising what
  a patient or clinician said, keep the relational layer (reassurance, praise, worry)
  alongside the clinical facts. Render every party's turns and let the clinician decide what
  matters.
- **Translate the act.** A statement about a symptom may be a request, a hint or a claim to
  be taken seriously.
- **Forms of address that reassure** in the source and would offend if carried over are
  replaced with a target form that reassures.

## 6. Research habits that pay here

- **Scale research to the term's role.** A term mentioned once needs a working definition; a
  key term developed through the text needs to be understood properly.
- **When the subject exceeds your knowledge, do not translate from the sentence alone.** Build
  the background first, from less specialised texts upwards.
- **A solution from a parallel text is a candidate.** Confirm it in a second independent
  text or with an expert. Frequency alone proves nothing.
- **Choose model texts written originally in the target language,** same genre, closest
  subject, recent.
