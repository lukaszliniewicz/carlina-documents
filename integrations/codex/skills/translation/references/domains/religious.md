# Religious and sacred texts

Scripture, liturgy, sermons, devotional and doctrinal writing, and texts about religious
traditions. The specific risks: the target language's religious vocabulary usually belongs to
one tradition and silently converts everything else into it; readers bring wording they
already know by heart; and helpful clarification becomes doctrine.

## 1. Whose vocabulary

- **Using one tradition's religious vocabulary for another tradition is an alignment
  decision.** "Church", "priest", "saint", "sin", "grace", "scripture", "prayer" carry a
  theology with them. Reusing a dominant confession's terms for a minority or unrelated
  tradition is right only where the brief is to bring the text close to those readers, and
  after checking for doctrinal confusion. Otherwise keep the tradition's own term and gloss
  it.
- **Words for the divine are not interchangeable.** Do not treat a supreme-being term or a
  divine name from one tradition as the equivalent of "God" or "god". Check what the source
  word means to its own community and what the target word will mean to these readers. Do
  not equate deities across pantheons from bilingual lists; verify roles and attributes.
- **Keep the plurality of divine names** a text uses, including names drawn from several
  traditions side by side. Collapsing them to "God" or "Lord" erases the point being made.
- **A ritual object, food or practice** is not named after a different religion's ritual
  that shares only a general category. Transliterate and gloss.
- **A borrowed local term used for a new concept needs explicit framing** that limits it to
  the intended sense. Never borrow silently.
- **Technical terms of a tradition are retained** (not sanitised into something more
  familiar, and not removed to make the tradition look more like, or less like, another).
- **Do not assume a tradition has one book that works like a Bible.** Establish which texts
  the community treats as authoritative and whether their role is ritual, popular or
  doctrinal before framing a text as "scripture".
- **Titles and ranks:** the standard dictionary equivalent often drops the rite or theology
  behind the term. Research it; gloss where the equivalent loses what matters.

## 2. Translating without expounding

- **Keep translation and exposition apart.** Translate what the passage says. Do not move
  from rendering sacred speech to explaining what it means unless the brief asks for
  commentary, and then mark the commentary as such.
- **Ambiguity stays open.** Where the source is ambiguous or inexplicit, do not resolve it
  towards any doctrine, including the readers' own. Where notes offer alternative readings,
  prefer wording that allows both.
- **Visions, dreams and symbolic prophecy:** translate the image. Do not substitute its
  interpretation, explain beyond what the original speaker explained, reorder events into an
  inferred chronology, or add when and how something was fulfilled.
- **Do not harmonise parallel passages.** Translate only what this passage says, even when a
  parallel text supplies a fuller phrase.
- **Do not weaken absolutes or declare hyperbole** because a claim seems impossible. "All",
  "never" and "none" stay as they are unless the text itself shows figurative use.
- **Unflattering or frightening depictions of the divine are preserved.** Do not upgrade fear
  to anger or retreat to heroism to protect dignity. Transgressive, sexual or violent
  passages are translated in full.
- **Language about the body of God** follows the theology stated in the brief (literal, or
  paraphrased as power and presence). Record which and apply it throughout.
- **Gendered language for the divine** is not fixed by a simple swap. Any alternative has
  theological consequences and may be unusable in worship; raise it as a decision.
- **Figurative or literal?** An image word (lamp, horn, cloak) is decided from its passage.
  An extended metaphor is kept where readers will recover its point; where they would take
  it literally, signal the comparison once at the start.
- **Doctrinally motivated changes are an adaptation.** If content is altered to satisfy a
  community's teaching, that is declared, and the faithful rendering and the added framing
  are kept in separate, marked layers.

## 3. Key terms, names and formulae

- **Two legitimate policies, chosen by the brief.** For study, interfaith or scholarly use:
  keep key source-language sacred terms visible beside their renderings, with notes that
  locate them. For a first or common-language version meant for ordinary reading: express
  the meaning in existing everyday words, without transliterations, coinages or new senses
  for known words. Do not mix the two.
- **Where no target term covers a key term's range,** do not substitute a fluent familiar
  word. Retain the source term, especially when it carries several equally important senses.
- **One fixed equivalent for each symbolic keyword** across the whole work, and identical
  wording for identical formulaic descriptions wherever they recur.
- **Mantras, seed syllables, ritual formulae, efficacious utterances and potent divine names
  are not translated.** Transliterate, preserving sound and sequence, and add a gloss where
  the reader needs one.
- **A sound, alphabet or wordplay device that carries doctrine:** keep the transliterated
  word beside the translation and explain the second reading in a note.
- **Scripture quoted as scripture is quoted from the version the readers use,** verbatim.
  Do not retranslate, paraphrase or switch versions midway. Check how earlier translations
  have fixed the wording in the target culture: expectations were formed by them, and a
  departure is read as a statement.
- **Historical referents stay historical.** Do not modernise objects, offices, money or
  measures. Ancient money is given as the term itself, a description, or a wage equivalent,
  never as a modern amount. Calendar references may be clarified by season.
- **Names of peoples and respected figures:** check that the target term does not downgrade
  them (sages into diviners, a people into a tribe).

## 4. Register, form and source

- **Register follows the genre and the occasion.** Do not replace a sacred act or phrase with
  a familiar everyday one where that trivialises it; equally, colloquial preaching to a
  mixed congregation is rendered colloquially and kept simple, not raised to a formal
  register. A consecrated wording used for memorisation or ritual is preserved as it is.
- **Tone is a high-consequence decision.** Whether a passage reads as parable, instruction,
  legend or report carries its credibility. Take it from the text's function.
- **Assign the text type per passage, not per canon.** A collection contains narrative, law,
  poetry, proverb and exhortation, and each needs its own method.
- **Recited or chanted verse** keeps short lines matched to the original's breath units. Do
  not expand it into prose paragraphs.
- **Borrowed religious words archaic in the target** are contamination unless the source is
  itself archaic for its readers (see `words.md`, residue checks).
- **Follow the source the tradition treats as authoritative.** That may be a mediating
  version or a commentary tradition and not the oldest text, unless the brief asks for a
  return to the root text. Where root text and authoritative commentary diverge, render the
  root and give the commentary's reading alongside. Where manuscripts differ, record which
  reading you followed.
- **Structural markup in scripture files** (chapter, verse and paragraph markers) is
  preserved exactly and in place.
- **A source account that conflicts with the readers' own tradition is not adjusted** to
  match their expectations. Prepare them in an introduction or note.
