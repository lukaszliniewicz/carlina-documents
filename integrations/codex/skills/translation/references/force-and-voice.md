# Force, stance and voice

What a text does to its reader beyond what it states: requests and refusals, politeness,
who is responsible, what is implied, who is speaking and how they sound. Fluent, accurate
translations go wrong here most quietly, because every sentence still looks correct.
Read for dialogue, correspondence, speeches, journalism and opinion, marketing, and any text
in which people address or describe each other.

## 1. The act performed

- **Translate the act, not the sentence type.** Name what each reader-directed sentence does
  (requests, warns, reassures, concedes, refuses) and keep that. A suggestion must not become
  a request, nor a request an order. A reassurance sets an objection aside; rendered as a
  prohibition it becomes a pre-emptive denial. Where the source's sentence type does not
  perform the same act in the target (a question used as a command, an imperative used as a
  blessing or a condition), change the type.
- **Formulas do not transfer.** A phrase that conventionally means something else in the
  source ("Correct me if I'm wrong", ironic praise, a question that is really a complaint) can
  invert its attitude when rendered literally. Find the target's own formula for that function.
  The same word can perform several acts: "sorry" may be apology, request to repeat, sympathy
  or condolence, and each has a different rendering.
- **Read the reply before translating the opening.** Whether a greeting-question is a formula
  or a real question, and how formal an exchange is, shows in what comes back.
- **For a quoted passage, name its cumulative force** (warning, plea, refusal) and let the
  reporting verb carry it where the target genre expects more than "said".

## 2. Politeness: neither softened nor sharpened

The two opposite errors are both common, and the second is the typical one for a model.

- **Do not soften.** Reproduce the source's level of threat in insults, rudeness, profanity,
  complaints, refusals and demands. Keep every element of a parallel insult and keep the
  concrete image; an abstraction is a softening. In business correspondence a softened
  complaint changes the act, and the recipient under-reacts to a message meant to make them
  act. Vocabulary that marks a dialect or sociolect is not obscenity and must not be rendered
  in the target's coarsest register; equally, real coarseness stays coarse.
- **Do not strip mitigation.** Justifications, modal and indirect forms, hesitations and
  hypotheticals around a request or criticism do work. An indirect, mitigated request reduced
  to a bare imperative is a new face threat, not a neutral shortening. Hedges and small talk
  that build trust or reduce a power gap look empty and are the first things cut under length
  pressure; check characterisation and relationship before deleting any of them.
- **Do not add mitigation.** A hedge, softener or vaguer word in the target with no source
  counterpart is a defect unless the brief calls for it.
- **Match the effect, not the number of markers.** "Please", "thank you", honorifics and
  softeners differ in frequency, force and position across languages. Render the degree of
  politeness and formality that the target uses for that situation and genre; a marker-for-
  marker copy is too effusive in some languages and curt in others.

## 3. Agency, certainty and who is included

- **Keep the distribution of agency and certainty.** Where the source keeps the agent out
  (passive, "there is", impersonal "it", a nominalisation such as "management decided" for
  "someone decided") or hedges with an evidential ("it is anticipated"), do not supply a named
  subject or a stronger modal for the sake of a smoother sentence. A missing agent is content:
  ask whether the omission does work (avoiding responsibility, keeping a role vague). If
  target grammar forces an agent, choose the most indefinite one.
- **"We", "they" and "our" assign membership and blame.** Check each against whom the source
  includes. Do not turn a passive into an active that supplies a "we", "they" into "we", or
  "our nation" into the country's name; the last turns identification with the audience into
  a statement about a country. Where the target distinguishes inclusive from exclusive "we",
  decide each occurrence.
- **Position signals weight.** Note which party the source keeps out of the opening, topical
  position and tucks into a prepositional phrase. A reordered or active-voice target that
  promotes that party reverses the emphasis.
- **Opinion dressed as fact.** Impersonal constructions, nominalisations and unattributed
  assertions present a view as given. Remove them and the text becomes neutral reporting; add
  hedges and the claim is weakened. Keep the device.

## 4. What is left unsaid

- **Deliberately open meaning stays open.** Hints, irony, rhetorical questions,
  understatement, indirect dialogue and riddles must not be resolved into one explicit
  statement for clarity. Do not spell out implicatures that create intimacy, suspense or
  ambivalence, and check that your wording does not admit a reading the source excludes.
- **What may be left implicit:** an element the reader can recover from general knowledge or
  from *earlier* text, never from later text. Where recovery is doubtful, make it explicit.
- **Presupposition check.** Everything the target presents as already known must be known to
  the target reader, from earlier in the text or from common knowledge in their culture.
  Supply what they cannot presuppose; cut what they do not need.
- **Details that are signals.** When a description, a small fact or a bland remark is there to
  make the reader infer something (about a person, a motive, a situation), state that
  inference to yourself and check that the rendering still supports it. If the target reader
  would infer something else or nothing, the passage is mistranslated however accurate its
  words.
- **Information needed to draw an inference goes in the text, not in a note.** A note
  interrupts the inference the writer designed.
- **Unstated premises.** Arguments often omit a premise the source readership shares. If the
  target readers may not share it, decide whether to leave it implicit (weaker for them) or
  make it explicit, and note the decision.
- **Resolve minimally.** When a reference must be resolved to make a sentence intelligible,
  choose the nearest plausible referent and the smallest context that works, not the richest
  available story.
- **A euphemism remains a euphemism.** Where wording disguises something unpleasant, the
  reader's seeing through it is the point. Do not translate it into the plain term; where the
  source's euphemism does not exist in the target, use the target's own for the same taboo.
- **Irony.** When the literal sense would praise what the text plainly condemns, test for
  irony. Carry the attitude; if the target language does not signal irony by asserting the
  false thing, use another mechanism (visible overstatement plus an evaluative comment that
  only makes sense in the writer's voice) instead of translating the assertion or labelling it.

## 5. Small words that carry the logic

- **Check the procedural words even when the content words are right:** tense and aspect,
  negation, determiners, connectives, discourse markers, word order. They tell the reader how
  to connect and weigh the content, and they are where errors survive.
- **Sentence adverbials with no content of their own** (after all, indeed, actually, anyway,
  now, of course): work out the relation each creates between two statements, and verify that
  the target connective creates that relation and is not merely fluent.
- **Concession signals.** Sentence-initial "certainly", "of course", "admittedly", "to be
  sure" followed later by "but" usually set up a view to be overturned; they are not the
  writer's conviction. The reader must feel it being set aside. The same opener can be a plain
  affirmation in another language, so do not carry it across by form.
- **Do not strengthen the view under attack.** When rendering a thesis the writer is about to
  demolish, keep it slightly distanced. If it sounds fully endorsed, the rebuttal loses its
  force and the reader cannot tell what the writer thinks.
- **Evaluation that does not fit the passage is deliberate.** Plain background that is heavily
  charged signals a writer expecting a doubting reader. Preserve the excess; do not normalise
  it towards the passage's apparent function.

## 6. Orientation: person, place and time

- **Fix the form of address for the whole text and never mix forms within it.** Then check
  every imperative, possessive and verb form; output drifts towards the most frequent pattern.
  Choose formal or informal from the actual relationship and attitude of the speakers
  (authority, age, familiarity, hostility), not from rank alone.
- **Convert modes of address to the target system** instead of translating them (title plus
  surname, first name plus patronymic, role title). Check the addressee's origin before
  choosing a title.
- **Re-anchor what points to the original readers.** "We", "here", "this country", "abroad",
  "come", "today", "last year": a literal copy tells new readers they belong to a community
  they do not, or states something false. Give dates explicitly, and judge distances,
  directions and the prominence of places from where the target reader stands.
- **Keep the frame of quoted speech.** Do not change the person a speaker uses for himself
  or herself because a reporting verb follows; the reader must know whose voice it is.
- **Unknown gender is not masculine.** When the source does not specify a person's sex and the
  target must, establish it from the text or the facts, use a neutral or inclusive
  formulation, or translate and flag the assumption.

## 7. Voice and register

- **Dialect or register?** Classify each non-standard or distinctive feature. Features of the
  *user* (region, class, generation, an individual's speech) belong to the person: carry them
  over as a comparable marker or say openly that they were lost. Features of the *use*
  (register, formality for the occasion) are re-matched to what the target situation calls for.
- **The intelligence test for speech.** After translating what a real or fictional person
  says, ask whether your version makes them sound less intelligent, less coherent or more
  alien than the source does, or than their own community would find them. If so, rebuild the
  register, syntax or idiom.
- **Demographic drift.** In first-person and opinionated text, ask whether the target would
  be attributed to the same kind of speaker: same apparent age, gender and social position.
  Machine translation shifts voices systematically towards older and more male.
- **Do not upgrade.** In popular, journalistic or conversational prose, resist the pull
  towards formality and precision: do not replace everyday approximations with exact technical
  terms, flatten figurative phrasing, or remove direct address. Set vocabulary level per text,
  including deliberately simple vocabulary where the voice requires it.
- **Do not enliven.** Where the source is a bare enumeration or colourless administrative
  prose, keep it bare: no livelier verbs, no added connectives for flow. The dullness is the
  register.
- **Informal and emotional features:** keep them when the text is published as the kind of
  text it is; drop them only when the translation is raw material for extracting facts.
- **Force carried by form stays in the form.** A triplet at a climax, repeated phrasing, a
  clause where a list would be tidier: keep three items, not two, and keep the pattern even at
  some cost to wording. But judge a device by its rank in the target: one that is marked in
  the source culture may be the unmarked default in the target and produce no effect, or the
  wrong one (close repetition is neutral emphasis in English and impassioned appeal in Arabic).
- **Words whose meaning is social** (swear words, intensifiers, greetings, forms of address):
  do not translate the denotation. Reconstruct who speaks to whom, at what distance and
  status, and choose the target item that evokes that situation.
- **Register can change within a document.** In public-service and institutional texts an
  informative surface often conceals an instructing or controlling passage. Reassess passage
  by passage.
- **A supplied style guide or glossary overrides your own preferences** wherever it speaks.

## 8. Testing for a systematic shift

When no sentence is mistranslated but the text feels different, look for small changes that
cluster on one dimension and all push the same way: more direct, less formal, more personal,
more certain, more polite. Treat the cluster as one fault, an unjustified filter laid over
the text, and correct it as a whole. Smoothing, explicitation and normalisation are
interventions too; audit them with the same scrutiny as deliberate additions or omissions.
