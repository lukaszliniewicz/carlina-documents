# Arabic notes

Into Arabic from English and out of Arabic. The points on text organisation come from Arabic-English discourse studies and translation textbooks; the typographic ones from subtitling style guides for Modern Standard Arabic, which a client's guide overrides. This is not a complete style guide for Arabic. Check Arabic examples in a right-to-left aware viewer before copying them.

## Punctuation and typography

- **Do not let punctuation carry cohesion or voices.** Arabic punctuation has little
  functional load, so mark the journalist's voice in the wording, with reporting
  verbs. Test by deleting every comma, bracket, dash and quotation mark; if the
  sense falls apart, rework the syntax.

## Numbers, dates and units

- **Spell out cardinals one to ten with agreeing nouns.** Write numerals for times
  and dates, spell out single-digit ordinals (الموسم الأول), and set two-digit
  ordinals with a kashidah (القرن الـ15). A bare digit cannot carry case and number:
  خمس سيدات, not 5 سيدات.

## Grammar carried over from the source

- **Choose verb-first or noun-first order per clause, by function.** The nominal,
  subject-first pattern carries tone-setting, evaluative and argumentative moves in
  Arabic; the verbal, verb-first pattern carries scene-setting narrative and
  exposition. A switch marks a structural move. If you cannot state a reason for
  the pattern, the choice is wrong.
- **Keep the masculine plural as the unmarked form.** It covers a mixed or unknown
  group; the feminine plural asserts an all-female group, so do not render an
  English 'they' with a feminine Arabic form unless the co-text establishes that
  every referent is female.
- **Track participants with pronominal suffixes, not free pronouns.** Arabic carries
  person, number and gender on the verb, noun and preposition, so keep an
  independent pronoun for emphasis and contrast only. A thematic line built on a
  repeated 'I' has to be rebuilt around processes.

## Address, politeness and tone

- **Use the target culture's own formula in correspondence.** Formulaic politeness is
  a genre convention, not a phrase to match; Arabic formal letters close with wa
  tafadalu biqbuul fa'iq al-ihtiraam ('be kind enough to accept our highest
  respects'), which does not correspond to 'Yours faithfully'.

## Sentences and text organisation

- **Turn bare juxtaposition into an explicit Arabic connective.** Supply the concessive
  or substantiating device the argument needs instead of translating the source's
  absence of a marker. A list of failures after 'These strengths are being wasted.'
  opens in Arabic with a concessive clause.
- **Merge short sentences and reduce connective variety.** Short sentences, an unusually
  varied array of conjunctions and missing wa and fa are the signature of
  interference from English; restore the ordinary particles. A press release uses
  wa, hatha-wa and kama with fewer sentence breaks than its source.
- **Into English: split Arabic's long one-sentence paragraphs.** Arabic leaves many
  relations to inference and its few conjunctions cover many meanings, so add
  English connectives and break the chunk.
- **Into Arabic: mark appended background detail with the formula.** Without wa
  jadiirun bi-dhikr ('it is worth mentioning') it reads as part of the main report.
  Coming out of Arabic, test whether the formula is empty or evaluative, then drop
  it or keep its slant.
- **Read an English certainty opener with 'but' as a concession.** 'Certainly', 'of
  course', 'admittedly' and 'indeed' set up a thesis to be overturned, so translate
  so the reader feels it set aside. Going into English, an Arabic opener of this
  shape states commitment, so render it as plain affirmation.
- **Do not keep direct quotation by default in Arabic news.** Indirect reporting is the
  norm: convert quotes and open each reported sentence with a verb of saying ('he
  said', 'he added'); keep first person only where the passage manages rather than
  informs. Coming out of Arabic, drop the chain of 'he added'.

## Names, terms and abbreviations

- **Handle acronyms by recognition, not by rule.** Translate those with a well-known
  Arabic equivalent, transliterate those the audience knows by sound (OPEC as أوبك,
  UNICEF as يونيسف), and quote the transliteration with a short descriptive word
  where no equivalent exists. Never coin a new Arabic abbreviation.
- **Reverse the name order of family-name-first languages.** Arabic readers take the
  first element as the given name, so Park Shin-Hye becomes شن هي بارك. Do not
  transliterate a Latin-only nickname: MBS becomes محمد بن سلمان.

## Subtitles and dubbing

- **Use Modern Standard Arabic only.** Where no MSA word exists, take the closest MSA
  equivalent by meaning rather than importing a dialect word, even a widely
  understood one; a dialectal exclamation reads as an error in a formal script.
- **Repeat the conjunction before each coordinated item.** A comma-only list is an
  English pattern and can be misparsed as apposition:
  المدونون والمترجمون والمترجمون الشفويون. A conjunction left at the end of a subtitle
  reads as a broken fragment, so start the next subtitle with it.
- **Do not cut an Arabic word in half.** Where the audio is interrupted, end at a word
  boundary and mark the break with an ellipsis, keeping a partial word only where
  the character visibly changes their mind mid-word:
  'يا لك من وقـ… أمرك يا سيدي.'
- **Open and close a quotation once across subtitles.** Use brackets rather than nested
  quotes for a name inside a quotation, and attach the article with a kashidah
  (الـ"برونكس") so it stays outside the marks.

## Translating out of Arabic

- **Do not normalise marked Arabic word order.** Reordering is deliberate and
  context-dependent; identify what the departure does (emphasis, topic shift,
  rhetorical effect) and reproduce that effect, or keep the departure and flag it.
- **Distinguish systemic from functional repetition.** A cognate accusative standing in
  for an adverb (wa nafaa ... nafyan qaaTi'an, 'categorically denied') carries no
  rhetoric and may become an ordinary adverbial; repetition in committed discourse
  states a commitment and must keep its force, as do parallelism and hyperbole in a
  through-argument.
