# French notes

Into French, usually from English, and out of French. The typographic conventions come from subtitling style guides and a client's guide overrides them; the legal points come from Canadian and European bilingual drafting; the rest are points about the pair from translation textbooks. This is not a complete style guide for French.

## Punctuation and typography

- **Into English, re-derive every mark from the target language.** Do not carry the source's
  quotation marks, question mark, apostrophe direction, spacing before punctuation,
  separators or currency position across. fr>en (Canada): «…» becomes quotation marks, the
  space before a colon goes, and 4 000,21 $ becomes $4,000.21.
- **Write « … » rather than English double quotes.**

## Numbers, dates and units

- **Write numbers the French way.** Use a comma decimal separator, a space before the unit
  or percent sign, and a space inside four-digit numbers except years; write time as '14 h'
  but '14h10', and spell out currency (3 000 pesos) unless space forces the symbol. French:
  'Il y avait 1 900 soldats en 1940.'

## Capitalisation, spelling and word formation

- **Capitalise people by nation, continent or city, not by race.** Write un Français, une
  Européenne, les Parisiens, but un homme noir; the named community is a proper designation,
  the racial adjective an ordinary descriptive word.
- **Write acronyms without periods, spaces or accents in capitals.** Capitalise an acronym
  pronounced as a word that is a proper name of more than four letters, lowercase one that
  has become a common noun and add a plural -s where French requires it. French: 'Unesco'
  but 'le sida', 'les ovnis'.
- **Give a woman the feminine job title.** Use the masculine only where the period setting
  needs it. -eur becomes -euse or -eure, -teur becomes -teuse or -trice, écrivain becomes
  écrivaine, chef becomes cheffe; the -esse form is outdated. French: 'la réalisatrice', 'la
  cheffe', 'une auteure/autrice'.

## Grammar carried over from the source

- **Do not turn a neutral plural into a feminine one.** A masculine plural covers a mixed or
  unknown-sex group and a feminine plural asserts an all-female one, so render 'they' as ils
  unless the source or co-text establishes that every referent is female.
- **Replace the possessive with the definite article when obvious.** French marks possession
  only when it is informative, and the literal possessive sounds wrong even where the
  grammar is right. en>fr: 'je quitte la maison vers 8 heures', not 'je quitte ma maison'.
- **Add marked morphosyntax to carry the level of speech.** Read the source level from its
  marked lexis and grammar, then choose French marked forms of determiners, pronouns, tense,
  negation or interrogation: formal ne alone, past historic, inversion, or informal pas
  without ne, on for nous.

## Words and phrases

- **Use the word ordinary readers use.** Where the brief puts a lay readership first, the
  everyday term beats the one dictionaries prefer, and note the deviation from the
  prescriptive norm. EN 'sexual abuse': abus sexuel, what everybody says, over agression
  sexuelle.
- **Test an idiom, never trust a lookalike.** Compare meaning, connotation and the subjects
  and situations each permits before adopting a target idiom. en>fr: 'Has the cat got your
  tongue?' against donner sa langue au chat ('give up'); tirer la jambe means 'drag one's
  steps'.

## Address, politeness and tone

- **Map tu and vous before you start.** Decide once per pair of speakers and revise the map
  when the relationship changes; the choice characterises silently. en>fr, Crooked House:
  'Darling – don't you understand?' becomes 'Mais vous ne comprenez donc pas?'

## Sentences and text organisation

- **Vary the wording of a repeated concept in French news.** Do not reuse the same noun
  phrase twice running: use a metonym, epithet or synonym on later mentions, and do not
  enforce terminology consistency here. 'le dollar américain' -> 'le billet vert'.

## Software and technical texts

- **Make we and you impersonal.** Convert English engagement markers to impersonal
  constructions in French instruction to raise formality and general validity.

## Legal and official texts

- **Reorder the provision to the target's legislative pattern.** English puts the conditions
  first and the rule after; French states the general principle first, often with the verb
  fronted. Quebec Civil Code art. 900: 'Sont immeubles les fonds de terre...'.
- **Write universal provisions impersonally in French.** Use il est interdit for 'no person
  shall', il peut for 'every person may', quiconque for 'every person who', Nul n'est
  inhabile for 'a person is not incompetent', and in modern drafting le fait de or a fronted
  est coupable ... quiconque.
- **Keep the polarity of a provision.** A negative provision stays negative; change only the
  mode of negation, negating the verb in French where English negates the subject. Official
  Languages Act s. 20(4): 'No person shall...' / 'il ne peut...'.
- **Collapse common-law doubling.** Coupled synonyms become one word or one fixed target
  expression, and archaic referentials are dropped, made explicit or replaced by the
  target's possessive. en>fr: 'full and equal access' → 'l'universalité d'accès'.
- **Mark condition-list relations in the introductory line.** French drafting uses dans le
  cas suivant, selon le cas or dans l'un ou l'autre des cas suivants where English puts a
  connector before the last item. en>fr, Official Languages Act s. 58(4).
- **Decide bare plurals and articles deliberately.** Inserting the definite article can
  convert an unspecified quantity into a totality and change the obligation; keep the
  source's indeterminacy and flag the choice. UNSC Resolution 242: 'from territories' became
  'des territoires'.
- **Treat look-alike legal terms as false friends until checked.** jurisprudence is only
  case law in French, la doctrine is legal scholarship, notaire is not 'notary' and
  magistrat is not 'magistrate'. fr>en: la doctrine → legal writing, not 'doctrine'.
- **Into English, use one word for cour, tribunal and juridiction.** The generic 'court'
  covers all three, les juridictions becomes 'courts and tribunals', and juridiction
  d'instruction is 'judicial authority'. fr>en: les juridictions françaises → 'the French
  courts and tribunals'.
- **Into English, re-punctuate and distrust identical comma placement.** The same commas in
  both versions are a symptom of syntactic calque. fr>en, Civil Code of Quebec Art. 2087
  needs two commas against the French four.

## Translating out of French

- **Search for the error patterns frequent in this pair.** Keep a per-pair list and target
  it rather than reading at random; French 'en + present participle' is routinely rendered
  'by + -ing', falsely asserting manner or means. fr>en: 'by presenting the historical
  context' adds a means the source did not have.
- **Do not read impersonal on or Rappelons as 'we'.** In French academic prose they are
  linking devices or refer to the surveyed party, a general practice or an unnamed agent;
  'we asked our respondent to specify...' attributes the surveys to the author.
- **Compensate lexically for what French grammar marks.** Choose Latinate or Greek lexis for
  formality (abolish for do away with), but do not stack uncommon or regionally marked
  informal English to mimic ordinary French informality.
- **Drop French connectives and incompleteness markers that only keep the text moving.**
  English usually runs on without them and leaves incompleteness implicit. Where the
  connective carries the argument it stays. So drop 'Ainsi' as 'Thus' and 'entre
  autres choses' as 'Amongst other things'.
