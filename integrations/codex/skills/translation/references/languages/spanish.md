# Spanish notes

Into Spanish and out of Spanish. The typographic conventions come from subtitling style guides and a client's guide overrides them; the rest are points about the pair from Spanish translation textbooks and studies of machine output. Settle the regional variety before anything else: several conventions below differ between Spain and Latin America. This is not a complete style guide for Spanish.

## Punctuation and typography

- **Put the full stop outside the closing quotation mark.** Spanish attaches outer punctuation
  to the sentence, not the quotation, and adds a period after a closing ? or ! as well: 'Él me
  dijo: "Regresa mañana".' and 'Me preguntó: "¿Me quieres?".'
- **Never combine question and exclamation marks,** and keep the inverted opening mark. Write
  '¿Cómo dices?' or '¡Cómo dices!', not '¡¿Cómo dices?!'. A closing ? or ! takes no full stop
  directly after it ('¿Cómo dices?.' is wrong); the full stop appears only after a closing
  quotation mark, as above.
- **Use single quotation marks for a gloss of meaning.** Reserve double marks for quoted speech
  or text: 'Gritan "Sãso", que significa 'libertad'.'
- **Re-punctuate commas instead of copying the source's placement.** Spanish commas follow
  semantic and rhythmical criteria, English and German ones formal-syntactic rules.

## Numbers, dates and units

- **Choose number, currency and time formats by region, not language.** Spain writes '2,50',
  '18:00' and '3000 €'; Latin America writes '2.50', '6:00 p. m.' and '$3000'.

## Capitalisation, spelling and word formation

- **Reduce capitals to Spanish convention.** Do not copy the source's capitals for titles,
  professions, months, days or common nouns in headings: 'El Alcalde insistió' becomes
  'El alcalde insistió'.
- **Where the source marks non-binary gender, carry the marking.** Use the form the client's
  guide names and do not normalise to the masculine. Subtitling guides have specified -x
  (amigx) and rejected -@, which still combines only masculine and feminine. Usage is
  unsettled, so say which form you used.
- **Reject a loan word or calque Spanish does not need.** Do so when Spanish already has a
  word, when the loan is recent and replaceable, or when it strains Spanish spelling: 'item'
  gives way to 'elemento', 'brainstorming' to 'lluvia de ideas'.

## Grammar carried over from the source

- **Identify what an English -ing form does before translating it.** As subject it takes an
  infinitive, as noun modifier an adjective or relative clause, only as adverbial a gerund:
  'Participating schools this year are:' is 'Las escuelas participantes este año son:'.
- **Render scheduled futures with the Spanish simple future.** Use it for English 'be + to' and
  progressive forms: 'The Council is to cover all requests' becomes 'El Ayuntamiento cubrirá
  todas las peticiones', not 'estarán recibiendo'.
- **Break up chains of 'de'.** A run of de-phrases reproduces an English noun cluster and
  leaves the relation between the nouns ambiguous: 'las peticiones de becas de comida de parte
  de familias en la lista de espera' needs each relation spelled out.
- **Resolve gender from the co-text, not from a stereotype.** Read back through the preceding
  sentences before writing any agreeing form: 'Hasanova era la brigadiera capo'. If the marker
  falls elsewhere, move the information, do not invent it.

## Words and phrases

- **Treat a cognate as suspect in either direction.** A shared form says nothing about shared
  meaning, so check what the target word means on its own: 'opacos' is 'gloomy', not 'opaque'.
- **Keep the force of a manner-laden verb.** Do not flatten specific verbs into one general
  one: 'homes have been lost, jobs shed, businesses shuttered' loses all three when rendered
  with forms of perder.
- **Check the clinical concept, not the resemblance.** English 'anthrax' is Spanish 'carbunco',
  and 'ántrax' is 'carbuncle'; clinical 'evidence' is proof (pruebas) and 'fatal' is lethal
  (mortal), not 'evidencia' and 'fatal'.

## Sentences and text organisation

- **Check hedges against the evidence in research prose.** English articles hedge more than
  Spanish ones, and a Spanish modal often intensifies where the English cognate would hedge.
  Do not map modals one to one in either direction; do not add or remove a hedge to suit
  the target genre without saying so.

## Names, terms and abbreviations

- **Abbreviate a title only immediately before a proper name.** Write 'la Srta. Henderson' but
  '¿Cuándo viene la señorita?'; a standalone abbreviation is wrong and reads as clipped.

## Legal and official texts

- **Use the institution's established official equivalent.** In WTO texts the parties are
  'reclamantes', not 'demandantes', and 'diferencia' is preferred to 'disputa' or
  'controversia', even when an ordinary word reads better.
- **Treat formal similarity as a warning in legal texts.** Check every cognate against the
  concept in this legal system: 'article' is 'sección', 'section' is 'artículo' of a law,
  'magistrate' is 'juez lego'.
- **Decide deliberately before inserting a definite article the source lacks.** It can turn an
  unspecified quantity into a totality and change the obligation; keep the indeterminacy and
  flag the choice. Resolution 242 reads 'de los territorios', the English 'from territories'.

## Subtitles and dubbing

- **Write neutral Spanish for interlingual subtitles.** Keep regionalisms in SDH of Latin
  American material: translation reaches many countries at once, so 'piscina' rather than a
  national variant; a transcription must preserve what the viewer hears, '¿pa qué?' included.

## Translating out of Spanish

- **Split long Spanish sentences and re-chunk for English.** Break multi-clause sentences,
  redistribute information across paragraphs, recast headlines and make background explicit:
  A headline such as 'Muere Narciso Yepes' becomes a noun phrase, not 'X has died'.
- **Do not treat elegant variation as terminology.** Check that each varied expression refers
  to the same thing before reading it as a synonym; 'Mercado Común' is not
  'Comunidad Europea', and reproducing the slip imports an error.
- **Name a paraphrase's relation with a connective.** Where Spanish achieves cohesion by saying
  the same thing in new words, English reads the new wording as new information: write
  'This means a decrease of 7.2 per cent' rather than a bare restatement.
- **Do not default to masculine when sex is unmarked.** Establish it from co-text or
  real-world knowledge, use an inclusive formulation, or translate and flag the assumption.
- **Resolve 'su' from sense, not proximity.** Spanish su can be 'his' or 'her'; in 'María
  llamó, pero Pepe no llamó. El sonido de su llamada me despertó.' only 'her call' is possible,
  though MT produced 'his call'.
- **Check the regional force of a vulgarism before rendering it.** coño is an extremely crude
  interjection in Cuba and a mild exclamation used by children in the Dominican Republic.
- **Hunt the errors that survive fluent drafting.** Check false friends, place names read as
  common nouns, word-for-word institutions and terms whose acceptability has changed:
  'conferencia' is 'lecture', 'en Aragón' is not 'at Aragon'.
- **Render the affect of a Spanish diminutive by other means.** Decide whether it marks small
  size or attitude; where it cannot be copied, use a compound adjective or added epithet
  ('tan solita': 'all on her own', not 'so alone').
- **Rebuild the connotation a prenominal adjective carries.** Adjective position is the main
  marker of evaluation in Spanish, so choose a stronger word or add an intensifier: 'el remoto
  ambito del Caribe' becomes 'the far distant confines of the Caribbean'.
