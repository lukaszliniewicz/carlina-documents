# Long documents and work in blocks

What changes when a text is too long to hold in view at once, is translated in blocks or
segments, over several sessions, or by several workers. The failures here are not in any one
block. They are decisions that drift, state that is lost at a boundary, and passes that get
skipped because each block looked finished. Read before starting any multi-block job and
again before the last pass.

## 1. Before the first block

- **Read the whole source once for orientation,** quickly, without solving anything. Note the
  voice, the hard passages, recurring wording, things set up early and used late. A machine
  draft or an earlier version makes this step feel unnecessary; it is the first one dropped
  and the errors it prevents are the text-level ones no block check finds.
- **A text that seems to raise no problems has not been read carefully.** Record the passages
  you expect to be difficult before drafting.
- **Write the style sheet before block one.** One place for the decisions that must hold
  everywhere: spelling variant, form of address, register, tense of narration, how names,
  titles, quotations and cross-references are handled, numbering, punctuation conventions,
  transliteration, recurring terms. Changing one of these halfway is a rewrite, not a
  replace-all.
- **For a stylistically demanding text, write the style analysis down** (register, sentence
  rhythm, rhetorical moves, voice) and carry it as a standing instruction. The impression
  left by the first pages does not survive fifty blocks.
- **Harvest terms before drafting,** not sentence by sentence while drafting. Settle
  equivalents for the recurring concepts first (see `names-terms-quotations.md`).
- **Decide a treatment per class of problem.** A genre predictably produces the same classes
  (proverbs, culture-bound items, wordplay, quoted verse, dialect). Decide how each class is
  handled once, so that block 40 does not solve differently what block 3 solved.
- **Research over the document, not the segment.** Many phrases that look idiomatic or
  untranslatable in isolation are explained a paragraph later.
- **Resolve open questions centrally.** Ambiguities and apparent source defects that affect
  several places are settled once, and the answer is pinned so that it applies identically
  everywhere. When work is distributed, settle them before distributing.

## 2. Size of the span

- **Translate in paragraphs or larger coherent stretches,** never sentence by sentence. A
  paragraph in one go gives fewer errors, including in who is speaking and what pronouns
  refer to.
- **Divide only at sentence boundaries,** preferably at paragraph or section breaks, into
  non-overlapping pieces.
- **More context is not always better.** An oversized span shows two symptoms: repetition (a
  word, phrase or sentence recurring) and a target much longer than its source. When either
  appears, shorten the span and redo the block from the source. Long spans also drop
  details, so run the completeness check on them.
- **Work in textual order.** Later decisions build on earlier ones, and solutions sometimes
  belong at a different point from where the problem appears.

## 3. State carried across blocks

Each block starts with less than the previous one ended with. What must cross the boundary:

- the style sheet and the glossary, including decisions made since the start;
- who is who: each person and thing being referred to, with gender, number, form of address
  and the name form in use;
- the tense and narrative position at the point where the last block stopped;
- open items: parked passages, unanswered queries, assumptions waiting to be confirmed;
- the last sentences of the preceding source and target, verbatim.

Rules:

- **After any interruption or boundary, re-read the immediately preceding source and target
  before continuing.** The first sentence of a block is where pronouns, connectives and
  tense go wrong.
- **Approved text feeds forward.** Once a block has been corrected, the corrected version is
  the context for the following blocks, not the first draft. Otherwise every fixed error is
  reintroduced downstream.
- **Fix classes, not instances.** When a defect recurs (a missing term, a systematic
  misreading, a formatting habit), add the term to the glossary or the rule to the standing
  instructions before the next batch, and sweep the blocks already done.
- **Do not stall.** When a passage resists, leave a marker that cannot occur naturally and
  will be found by search, translate on, and return once the rest is drafted. Later text
  often solves it.
- **Log decisions when they are made.** Keep a running record: quotations and references to
  verify, terms whose referent is unclear, spelling and hyphenation decisions, recurring
  terms with the equivalent adopted. Reconstruction afterwards does not work.
- **Record the reason for every non-obvious choice,** with the passage: "the source says X;
  rendered Y because Z". Without it the next pass, yours or another's, will reverse a
  considered decision.
- **Mark what has been verified** at the moment of verifying, so the final pass does not
  repeat checks, and does not skip ones it assumes were done.
- **A paraphrase is a tool for understanding, never a source.** Restating a hard sentence in
  your own words detaches the sense from the wording. Then translate from the original with
  that understanding. Whatever the paraphrase or a summary lost (connotation, emphasis,
  ambiguity) is lost from a translation made from it.

## 4. Reused material

An exact match from a memory, a glossary hit, a locked segment and your own rendering from an
earlier block all have the same status: a proposal. Before accepting one, check it in the
current sentence and against the neighbouring blocks: pronoun gender and number, tense, form
of address, term agreement, "this" and "here", names, numbers and factual detail. Know where
a suggestion came from, because that decides how to check it: a memory match is checked
against what differs from the stored source; machine output has no such guide and is checked
whole against the source. A weak fuzzy match is discarded, not edited.

## 5. Warning signs while drafting

- **The job is going fast and nothing has needed a decision.** Slow down and question the
  text deliberately. Fluency suspends monitoring.
- **A wording that surprises you by being striking.** Unpredictable continuations are
  high-risk: verify them against the source and the target's norms before keeping them.
- **Several hard decisions in one passage.** Difficulty clusters where syntax, style and
  terminology all need solving at once. After working through such a passage, go back over
  it for the small items passed over while the big ones were being settled.
- **A half-known word rendered vaguely.** Context supports a vague rendering of almost
  anything. Look the word up, then let context choose among what it can mean.
- **A point that turns on specialist doctrine you hold only in outline.** That is a research
  or query trigger, not a place to reason from plausibility.
- **Effort spread evenly.** Rank units by what an error there would cost the reader and the
  text's credibility, not by linguistic difficulty. Routine low-risk units get a competent
  first solution; conscious effort and checking go to the high-risk ones. For a consequential
  passage, write out two or three real alternatives and choose by criteria taken from its
  function; do not break the tie mechanically.

## 6. After the last block

A final whole-text pass is the other step that a block workflow suppresses. Each block was
checked, so the document feels checked. It has not been read.

1. **Return to every parked marker and open query.** None may reach delivery unresolved and
   unreported.
2. **Read the assembled target as continuous prose** (`sentences-and-text.md`, section 8),
   boundaries out of sight.
3. **Sweep consistency across the whole document:** names, nicknames and informal
   designations against their first occurrence; each recurring term with every form used for
   it; form of address; spelling variant; numbering and cross-references.
4. **Compare the renderings of passages that recur verbatim in the source** (boilerplate,
   repeated warnings, headings, refrains).
5. **Settle titles and headings last,** from what the body turned out to say, and check them
   against the terms used in the text and against the table of contents.
6. **Review coverage follows where the argument lives.** If the core content is concentrated
   (introduction, conclusions, recommendations), check those in full. If it is distributed,
   every section carries it and sampling is not a review.
7. **Revision effort follows weakness.** Blocks that were out of domain or drafted badly gain
   most from another pass. A strong block gains little and can get worse; leave it.

## 7. What to hand to the reviser

Whether the next reader is a person, another model or yourself in a later session:

- which passages were especially difficult, and why;
- which odd-looking renderings are deliberate;
- which terms and names were verified, and against what;
- what was assumed, what could not be resolved, and which source defects were found;
- which aspects of the source the brief led you to preserve and which to set aside.

Say where each one is. A flag without a location and a proposed replacement rarely gets
acted on: in one study every violated term was correctly flagged and only about six in ten
were then corrected.
