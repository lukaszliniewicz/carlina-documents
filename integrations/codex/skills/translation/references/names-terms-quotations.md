# Names, terms and quotations

Items with a correct answer that exists outside the text and cannot be worked out from it.
A fluent guess looks exactly like the right answer here, so the rules are mostly about where
the answer comes from and how to check it survived. Read for any text with proper names,
institutions, specialist vocabulary, a glossary, cited works or quoted speech.

## 1. Names

- **An institution's name is not the sum of its parts.** Do not translate an unfamiliar
  organisation, office or programme component by component: the result reads as a common-noun
  phrase and may name a different body. Find the established target name. If none can be
  established, keep the original and add a gloss.
- **Verify the spelling of every proper name against something outside the text.** A name that
  is also an ordinary word passes every spelling check and can still be wrong.
- **Fix one transliteration system for the document,** chosen by readership (popular press,
  bibliographic, scholarly), and override it only for names that already have a conventional
  spelling in the target.
- **Restore a foreign name to its own spelling.** A name that came into the source from a third
  language (an English surname in Cyrillic, a French town in Arabic script) goes back to its
  original form, never through a second transliteration. Check each name individually. Into
  the source's direction, use the established target form of the foreign name, not an ad hoc
  one.
- **Contested names are a stance.** Where a place, event or people has competing names, the
  choice takes a side. Reproduce the source's own term or follow the brief, and record the
  alternative. Quietly choosing the more neutral name is also an intervention.
- **A name whose meaning matters.** If it recurs, keep the name and gloss it once, in the text
  or a note. If it occurs once, translate the meaning and drop the name.
- **A nickname or allusive name that only works for source readers.** The literal equivalent
  says nothing. Either expand it with the minimum that makes it meaningful or replace it with
  the neutral official designation: expansion for readers who will study the text, the
  official name for quick reading.
- **Classifier for an opaque name.** When the context does not show whether a name is a
  person, a river, a town or a group, add the classifying word.
- **Explaining by comparison.** When you help an unknown referent across by likening it to a
  familiar one, verify that the two share the attribute the passage relies on. For an
  institution, check that the target-culture counterpart can actually do what the sentence
  says it does.
- **Allusions to national history or a stock cultural frame:** identify the specific referent
  before translating. A near-synonym with a different evaluative frame changes what is being
  claimed about it.
- **Do-not-translate items** (brands, product names, trademarks, identifiers, anything the
  brief lists): the string appears verbatim, even where a local form exists. After drafting,
  check each one character by character; they do not reliably survive.
- **Brands in a non-Latin-script target, with no instruction:** original spelling at the first
  and at prominent appearances (headline, legal notice). A transcribed form only later in
  running text, and only the single form already established among readers.
- **Titles and ranks.** Convert to the target's system of address (see `force-and-voice.md`).
  A title explained once can be dropped where it merely names the person, and kept where the
  speaker is using the office for effect.

## 2. Quotations and references

- **Never compose a citation, bibliographic reference, URL or attribution from memory.** This
  applies to translator's notes and research notes as much as to the translation. If you
  cannot point to it in the supplied material or a source you have actually checked, leave it
  out or mark it unverified.
- **A quotation you cannot locate does not stay in quotation marks.** Render it as indirect
  speech or flag it. Verify at the original source, not at a later text that cites it, and do
  not accept a memory match or recycled rendering for quoted material without checking the
  wording.
- **Text that already exists in the target language is reproduced, not retranslated.** When a
  legal or official text quotes, cites or amends an instrument with a published translation,
  use that wording verbatim, even where you would have written it differently.
- **Decide what each quotation is doing** (informing, expressing the author's view,
  persuading, invoking authority) and translate it for that job. They are not all citations
  requiring the same literalness.
- **A high-stakes direct quote you cannot render with confidence** becomes reported speech
  with a neutral reporting verb. A fluent direct quote that distorts a named speaker is the
  worse outcome.
- **Spoken quotations** may be tidied of hesitations and false starts where the genre does so.
  The substance is not altered.
- **An author's sceptical marker inside a quotation** (a bracketed question mark, a "sic", an
  interjected phrase) stays, attached to the element it doubts. Keep the quoted words
  plausible as something the quoted source could have written.
- **Direct or indirect speech follows the target's reporting norms.** Convert where the
  source's choice is unnatural in the target genre, adjusting pronouns and tense, without
  crossing the previous rules.
- **Titles of works.** Do not invent an "official" title. Use the published target title if
  you can verify one exists; otherwise keep the original and add a translation marked as
  yours. Translate your own document's title last, once the body has shown what it is about.

## 3. Choosing a term

- **Your first rendering is a hypothesis.** Draft a provisional solution from what you know,
  then confirm or replace it using monolingual target sources: reference works, original
  writing in the field, parallel texts. A bilingual dictionary alone confirms nothing.
- **Shallow knowledge of the field is a trigger for research, not a licence to improvise.**
  The plausible general-language rendering is the usual error.
- **The dictionary equivalent can name a different concept.** Where the standard equivalent
  belongs to another slot in the target's term system for this domain, it is a terminology
  error. Find the word a specialist would use; do not defend the dictionary word as a
  synonym.
- **Do not settle an equivalent by asking a model how a word is translated,** yourself
  included. The answer is a tidy closed list that does not match what translators and
  writers actually use. Look at attested usage for the same construction.
- **Between competing target forms,** prefer the one with a dictionary or termbase entry, then
  the one more frequent in the domain, then the one without avoidable surface variation
  (no needless hyphen or slash, no clipped form where the full term exists, no transliteration
  where a translation exists).
- **An entrenched loan stays.** In a fast-moving field, do not replace the loanword or acronym
  that professionals actually use with a descriptive native coinage merely because one exists.
  Check current target texts.
- **Test the chosen rendering at every occurrence.** Where it fails in some contexts, the
  source word covers more than one concept: split it into separate target terms, or use a
  longer phrase that covers all the senses.
- **A term that is wrong in the source** is a source defect to report, not a translation
  problem to solve by choosing a better target term silently.

## 4. Keeping terms consistent

- **Consistency is per concept, not per string.** Two target designations for one concept is
  an inconsistency even when each is acceptable. Decide first whether the source's different
  expressions are one concept. Expect a fair share of a term's occurrences to vary in form
  (shortened, expanded, reordered, coordinated, derived); those are the same term.
- **The client's term wins over a better one.** Where a mandated or house term exists for the
  concept, use it. Do not correct supplied terminology that looks substandard: keep it and
  flag it.
- **But a stored entry is not a decision about this sentence.** Before reusing a glossary
  entry, memory match or previous rendering, check that it is the same concept in this
  context. The client's term binds for the concept it was recorded for and no further.
- **A glossary in the prompt is advice the draft may have ignored.** After translating, check
  mechanically that each mandated term appears in the block where its source term occurs,
  allowing for inflection, and fix the block where it does not. Glossary support speeds work
  up without reducing terminology errors by itself.
- **Do not judge term correctness by ear.** A model's quality judgement is close to blind for
  terminology even when it is reliable on meaning. Compare against the glossary or the
  established equivalent, item by item.
- **Never replace every instance of a target word.** One target word may stand for several
  source expressions. Map each occurrence to its source before changing any.
- **What to record with a term:** the gender and citation form in a gendered language (a term
  stored without them takes the wrong agreement in every later block), its register and
  typical collocations, and when an alternative should be used instead. Record attested
  variants with their usage; do not pretend one concept always has exactly one form.
- **What belongs in a glossary:** not only technical vocabulary. Include the ordinary words
  that name the domain's documents, services and activities, and borrowings from adjacent
  fields; these drift most across a long text.

## 5. Acronyms

- **First mention in full with the acronym in brackets, the acronym afterwards.** If sections
  will be read independently (reference material, web pages, anything translated or displayed
  in fragments), expand again at the first occurrence in each section.
- **Do not decode an abbreviation the source leaves undecoded** unless you have verified the
  expansion. An invented expansion is an addition the reader cannot check.
- **Retained foreign acronyms:** give the full foreign term at first use and a translation
  where that helps. Use a target-language acronym only if it already exists; never coin one.

## 6. What counts as evidence

- **Frequency among translations may be a chain of copied errors.** Before adopting a
  rendering because it is common in a memory, a term database or search hits, check that
  original writing in the target language uses it. Counts can rule out a coinage nobody uses;
  they cannot choose between attested options that differ in connotation or level.
- **A similarity score measures strings.** A high match is evidence that two sentences look
  alike, not that the stored translation suits this one.
- **Read the hits before counting them.** Search engines ignore punctuation and
  capitalisation, add "similar" results and count duplicates. Confirm that the hits contain
  the string in the intended structure and sense.
- **In a bilingual concordance the source side is reliable and the target side is a guess.**
  The highlighted target stretch is aligned statistically and is often wrong. Read the whole
  target sentence yourself.
