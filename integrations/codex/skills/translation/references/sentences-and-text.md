# Sentences and text

Grammar the two languages do not share, the order of information, and what holds a text
together across sentences. Read when the source is syntactically dense, when sentences are
being restructured, and always for texts translated in blocks or segments.

## 1. When the target forces a choice the source did not make

Number, gender, formality, aspect, definiteness, kinship detail, whether an action was
completed: languages oblige speakers to state different things.

1. Take the value the surrounding text supports, looking beyond the sentence and into earlier
   sections.
2. If the text does not settle it, **recast so that the choice disappears**: a passive or
   impersonal construction, an infinitive, a plural, a role noun, dropping a possessive. This
   is better than picking a value and adding a caveat.
3. If neither works and it matters, pick the most probable value and **flag the assumption**,
   or ask. Never invent a value silently.
4. If a later passage reveals the value (a person's sex, how many there were), **go back and
   repair the earlier occurrences**. Do not leave them because they read acceptably.

Check long-distance agreement explicitly (a pronoun and an antecedent several clauses away).
Local fluency does not guarantee it.

## 2. Who does what to whom

- **In a long, embedded sentence, identify the grammatical subject of every clause before
  rendering any of it.** If the sentence cannot be carried over without muddling who does
  what, restructure or split it.
- **Track agency across stretches, not word by word.** Separate shifts the target grammar
  requires from optional ones; a series of optional shifts (active to passive, agent to
  circumstance) reframes responsibility even though each is defensible.
- **Nominalisation works in both directions, so mirror the source's level.**
  - Do not turn an action or commitment into a labelled topic: "ensuring that all girls are
    educated" is not "the problem of female education".
  - Do not unpack a source nominalisation or impersonal construction into a clause with an
    explicit agent and an "if": each unpacking raises the force, and a run of them turns a
    statement into a demand or a threat.
  - Do not add a causal connective that makes explicit an accusation the source only
    presupposed.
  Unpack only where the target cannot carry the abstract noun naturally, and then without
  adding an agent.
- **A passive must do the passive's job.** Use one in the target for the same purpose as in
  the source (focus on the receiver, agent unknown or withheld). A passive whose agent is
  named elsewhere in the same sentence hides nothing and only confuses.
- **After reordering, check what attaches to what.** Purpose, cause and result phrases must
  still belong to the right predicate; a displaced "thereby" clause can make an effect cause
  itself. After translating an adverb, verify what it now modifies: keeping "responsibly" but
  changing its scope produces a different claim.
- **Coordination is not subordination.** Do not turn two coordinated items or facts into a
  relative clause or apposition unless that relation really holds; the restructured sentence
  asserts something the source did not. The reverse also applies: an "and" can do emphatic
  work that a tidy subordinate clause loses.
- **Restrictive or not.** A relative clause that restricts makes its content a separate
  condition or instruction; a non-restrictive one presents it as following automatically.
  Match the source.
- **A missing object is not filled by the nearest noun.** When the source omits an argument
  the target needs, decide what it is from the situation and flag the inference if it is not
  obvious.

## 3. Time, modality, definiteness

- **Tense is a property of the passage.** After changing a verb form, check tense and aspect
  in the neighbouring sentences. This class of error is invisible when each sentence is
  compared only with its own source sentence.
- **Tenses used non-literally** (historical present, a past form for a certain future, a
  present for an imminent future): render the actual time reference with the tense the target
  normally uses for it, unless the device itself is an effect the target shares.
- **Modals, hedges and degree words are the strength of the claim.** "May", "should", "will",
  "possibly", "probably", "definitely": do not drop one, add one, or move it up or down the
  scale.
- **A definite article may point at something specific.** "The wall", "the speech", "the
  firefighter" may refer to one known thing; an indefinite rendering erases the allusion and
  stays grammatical.
- **"If" is not always hypothetical.** Where "if" introduces something the speaker holds
  certain, a literal conditional can make target readers infer doubt; where it introduces
  something the speaker knows to be false, a literal rendering can attribute a false belief.
  Render the status.

## 4. Order of information

- **The end of the clause carries the news.** Put what the reader already has first and what
  is new last. When you restructure a sentence into the target's normal order, look at what
  has landed in final position: a natural-sounding target may now end on a subordinate detail
  instead of the point.
- **Fronting gives only local prominence.** To make something prominent, change what is in
  final position. Front a non-subject only for contrast, or use a construction (a passive)
  that brings it forward without marking it.
- **Match markedness.** Use marked target structures (clefts, fronted complements, inverted
  order) only where the source is itself marked, and leave unmarked source clauses unmarked.
  For a cleft, work out which element it presents as new and keep that distribution, with
  whatever construction the target uses.
- **Topic markers are not always neutral.** Rendering a neutral topic-comment structure with
  "As for X" or "Concerning X" claims a contrast the source does not make.
- **An emphasiser can smuggle in a contrast.** "In reality", "actually", "in fact" imply
  "as opposed to appearance" and can take the focus from the word that should carry it.
- **Events out of order.** Where the source narrates events out of sequence and the target
  reader will take narration order as event order, mark the earlier event (tense, "before",
  "had") or reorder.
- **Marked source order has a job.** Before normalising a departure from neutral order, work
  out what it does (emphasis, topic shift, rhetorical effect) and reproduce that by the
  target's means.

## 5. Splitting, merging and sentence shape

- **Translationese is mostly structural.** Whole source constructions carried over unadapted
  outnumber literal phrases by about two to one. Rewording single items will not repair such a
  text; the sentence has to be rebuilt. But never invent a target construction to mirror the
  source: use only patterns the target has.
- **Split or merge only for a reason found in the text.** Prefer splitting to merging, never
  merge three or more sentences, and keep the sentence count close to the source. When
  splitting, group the words around each verb, then reconnect the new sentences with
  connectives that keep the original relations.
- **Excess words and stacked clauses** are usually the source's architecture surviving in the
  target. Recast instead of transcribing.
- **Paragraphing.** Printed paragraph breaks are a convention that differs between languages
  and publications. If the format allows re-paragraphing, place breaks where the rhetorical
  moves change. If source and target must stay aligned (bilingual layout, block-based tools,
  legal texts), keep the source's breaks.
- **Two patterns to check first in machine-drafted text:** coordinated series whose items are
  not grammatically parallel, and pronouns or tenses that change within a sentence or short
  passage.

## 6. Connectives

- **Preserve the relation, not the connector.** Decide what the source connective signals
  here: support for the previous claim, a conclusion, an objection, a reassessment of
  everything before. Then use whatever makes that relation clear in the target: add a
  connector where the source has none, drop one that would sound strange, or change it.
  When unsure, remove the connective, read the two statements bare, and name the relation that
  actually holds between them.
- **Changing a connective changes the argument.** Adding or removing one is not a matter of
  texture. Accept it only if you would defend the reasoning the passage now claims.
- **Events or argument?** A connector that links events in the world ("therefore the bridge
  collapsed") is different from one that organises the writer's case ("in short", "so"). Do
  not render a summing-up connector with a causal one because the causal reading is
  grammatical.
- **A connective with two readings propagates.** If a target connective can be adversative or
  concessive, the reading the reader takes affects how the neighbouring sentences are
  understood. Check it against them.
- **Do not paper over with adverbs.** If the source held a passage together by parallel
  sentence openings and repeated frames, restore that parallelism instead of adding "in this
  respect" or "therefore".
- **Counter-expectation markers one by one:** only, even, still, never, no longer, scarcely,
  rather, surely. They look like spare particles, carry the writer's stance, and are the items
  most often dropped. "Greatness is never a given" without "never" is another claim.
- **Cohesion or coherence?** If every connector and reference is correctly rendered and the
  passage still does not hold together, the fault is a step the target reader cannot
  reconstruct: a missing proposition, referent or assumption. More connectors will not fix it.

## 7. Reference chains

- **Resolve pronouns from the document, not the sentence.** Before rendering a pronoun, or a
  sentence opening with a verb and no stated subject, find its referent, in earlier sections
  if necessary, and carry its number, gender and animacy into the target. If it stays
  unclear, restructure or keep the form underspecified and flag it.
- **Track each participant through the whole text.** Passages that alternate between two
  referents need this most, because output drifts towards the more frequent pronoun form.
- **New or given must stay recoverable.** A repeated full name after introduction suggests a
  new person or special emphasis; an unclear pronoun after a gap or between two same-gender
  participants needs the noun back. Reintroduce a referent as new once it has been out of
  play for many sentences or a section break has intervened.
- **Every definite reference needs an antecedent in the target.** Check "this X", "the X",
  "such an X" especially after anything was cut, moved or merged.
- **Density of repetition follows the target's habits,** not the source's. Where the target
  cannot use a pronoun, move down this scale only as far as needed: repetition, synonym, more
  general term, general word. Each step adds meaning, and general words often add evaluation.
- **Announcements must be honoured.** When a text says "there are four factors" or "first the
  arguments for, then against", count the items and the sequence markers and check that the
  plan is delivered where promised.
- **Recurring formulas keep their rendering.** When an expression recurs, reuse the rendering
  already used, taken from a comparable position (a title, a step and a condition need
  different forms).
- **Theme test for a passage that will not flow.** List what each sentence starts from. If a
  starting point cannot be linked to something in the previous sentence, or a pronoun in that
  position presupposes something not yet given, the passage is ill-formed although each
  sentence is grammatical. If the source's pattern cannot be kept, build another consistent
  one (for example, every sentence starting from the place, or from the agent).

## 8. The assembled-text pass

After translating in blocks, segments, or from pre-translated material, read the assembled
target as continuous prose with the segment boundaries out of sight, and repair what only
shows across them: pronoun reference, "this" and "here", connectives, tense, form of address,
terminology, register, paragraph flow. Across every join with reused or previously approved
text, confirm that its pronouns have antecedents in the new context, that its tense, terms
and register match, and that its numbers, dates and names agree with this document and not
the one it came from. A locked or approved segment that breaks the grammar around it is
flagged, not silently reused.

Finish by reading the target alone and asking whether it forms its own chains of related
words and a single voice, or is a collection of locally accurate sentences.
