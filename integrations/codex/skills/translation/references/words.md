# Words, phrases and figures

Tests and decision rules for lexical choices. Read when word choice is the difficulty: loaded
or polysemous vocabulary, idioms and metaphors, culture-bound items, look-alike words, or a
draft that is accurate but flat or oddly coloured.

## 1. Fixing the sense

- **A look-alike is a candidate, not a decision, in both directions.** Use the cognate only if
  the target word covers the sense this context activates. Also check the target word's own
  range: a borrowed or cognate word may have senses the source word lacks, so a perfect-looking
  match can import a reading. But do not discard a cognate merely because it resembles the
  source; a replacement found by desperate search is often worse. Re-test it in context.
- **Substitution test.** Put the dictionary definition of the candidate sense into the
  sentence in place of the word. If the passage still does what it was doing, the sense is
  right; if the result is absurd or over-specific, it is wrong here. Run the same test on the
  target word you chose.
- **Look at what the word contrasts with.** List the neighbours in the source word's set
  (stroll, walk, march, trudge). The choice is right only relative to them. Where the target
  merges two members that the text distinguishes, rebuild the distinction by other means.
  Bilingual dictionaries are organised by target word boundaries and will not show a contrast
  that exists only in the source.
- **A half-known word is not settled by context alone.** Context tells you which feature of
  the word is needed here; it does not tell you what the word can mean. Establish the range
  first, then let context select.
- **Verbs of saying and showing.** Decide the sense of a speech-act verb from its complement:
  with a future action of the addressee it directs (suggest = propose); with a state of
  affairs it asserts (suggest = indicate, maintain). Keep the epistemic force of reporting
  verbs: never turn "suggests", "indicates" or "may" into "shows", "confirms" or "will", or
  the reverse.
- **Words for position, shape and arrangement.** Test the candidate against the actual object.
  If its image implies the wrong geometry (flat, inside, upright), it is wrong even when a
  dictionary lists it.
- **After solving a hard item, re-read the sentence for the small words beside it.** Attention
  fixes on the idiom or term, and "barely", "hardly", "only", "even", "still" get lost.

## 2. When there is no exact equivalent

- **Err towards the general.** A broader term loses a little information; a narrower term adds
  information the source does not contain, which is the worse error. Use a narrower term only
  when nothing else exists and the added detail is implied by the context. A change in
  specificity is an accuracy error even when the sentence reads well: "boy" rendered by a word
  for a small child, a particular kind of officer rendered as officers in general.
- **Reproduce only the features that matter here.** A rendering padded with every dictionary
  feature of the source word, or a compound that spells out a feature the context suppresses,
  misleads the reader.
- **When one general source noun maps to several specific target words,** resolve it from the
  situation or ask which is meant. Do not settle for a vague generic if the target must choose.
- **Change category instead of hunting synonyms.** When no word of the same kind works, render
  a person by the effect they have, an object by what is done to it, a state by its cause.
- **Keep the manner in verbs.** Where the source uses a specific, forceful verb (smash,
  shed, wield), use a target verb of comparable force. Do not render a varied series with one
  repeated general verb.
- **A one-item slot cannot hold an explanation.** In titles, headings, labels, slogans,
  captions and interface strings a paraphrase destroys what the item is for. Find a unit.
- **Check "untranslatable" words against this text.** Famous untranslatables often reduce to an
  ordinary phrase in context; another foreign word burdens the reader for nothing.

## 3. Attitude that travels with words

- **Words absorb the attitude of their usual company.** Two equivalents with the same core
  sense can differ in what they habitually sit beside (illness, blame, pressure). A neutral
  target word can lose the source's stance and a tainted one can invert it. Choose by that
  aura, not by denotation alone.
- **Polarity comes from the passage.** Establish whether an evaluative word praises or blames
  from the surrounding text, then check the polarity of your candidate too: some target
  epithets are ambiguous or have shifted in current use, and a technical-sounding positive
  word is a common wrong landing for a negative source word.
- **Mismatch in force is directional.** If the target word is more neutral than the source,
  you can add the force with a modifier or nearby. If it is more loaded, you cannot subtract;
  find another word.
- **Deletion test.** Delete the word and re-read. If the information is unchanged, the word
  carried only attitude or emphasis, and omitting it is a loss of force that needs a reason,
  not an economy.
- **Every strengthening needs a source.** No intensifier added to a bare evaluative adjective.
  No evaluation the source only evoked turned into an explicit epithet; that makes the
  judgement yours and tells the reader what to feel. A livelier synonym that imports an
  attitude makes the passage worse however well it reads.
- **Under condensation, attitude disappears first.** When shortening or summarising, check each
  epithet and the strongest loaded term individually; neutral content nouns survive on their
  own.

## 4. Collocation and frequency

- **Paraphrase the phrase before translating its words.** If the paraphrase says more than the
  sum of the dictionary senses (a dry voice is a cold one; to run a car is to own and maintain
  it), translate that meaning.
- **Conventional pairings are arbitrary and language-specific.** Take the verb the target
  conventionally uses with that noun; do not carry over the literal sense of the source verb.
- **Test an elegant synonym in the exact noun context.** Near-synonyms keep different company
  (rules are broken, regulations are not), so a swap can produce an impossible combination.
- **Odd sentence, every word correct?** Check whether the verb's subject or object belongs to
  the class the verb allows, and replace the verb instead of polishing the rest.
- **When the only idiomatic collocation shifts the meaning,** take it if the lost distinction
  is not in focus in the passage. If the argument depends on the distinction, keep the meaning
  and accept a less usual wording.
- **Grammatical is not the same as usual.** A construction that is correct but much rarer in
  the target than in the source reads as emphatic or marked where the source was neutral.
  Conversely, a wording is not wrong merely because a competitor is more frequent; to reject
  it you need a specific reason (wrong genre, region, medium).

## 5. Idioms and figures

- **Grade the figure first.** Dead or fossilised: use the target's plainest natural wording,
  and never revive the image with a vivid literal phrase. Stock: use the stock target
  equivalent. Original: only this earns an invented image. An unusual word combination in the
  target where the source was commonplace is the symptom of getting this wrong.
- **Decide by text type.** In an informative text give the sense in normal target wording. In
  a text valued for its form, keep the author's image, switching to a comparable target figure
  only when the literal becomes strained. In persuasive text choose the version with the
  strongest effect.
- **A target idiom must fit exactly or not be used.** One that merely means roughly the same
  brings its own attitude, force and range of situations ("crossing our arms" turns inaction
  into opposition). If any of these differ, give the plain sense. Never adopt a target idiom
  because it resembles the source idiom, and never one stronger or more specific than the
  original. Some written registers avoid idiom altogether even when one exists.
- **But notice what the plain sense removes.** If the source was idiomatic or figurative at a
  point where that matters (voice, humour, persuasion), check whether the target still has
  something of the kind there or nearby. If not, that is a decision to record, not a default.
- **A transparent literal image can stay.** If the figure is self-explanatory in translation,
  readers can understand and enjoy it.
- **Keep figures coherent.** Prefer an image from the imagery already running through the
  document. If you replace a figure that the source goes on developing, carry the replacement
  through every point where it is developed.
- **Figures that fail when rendered word for word:**
  - two words for one idea ("X and Y" where Y describes X): recast as modifier plus noun;
  - understatement by negation ("not unkind"): render the intended positive where the negation
    would weaken or confuse;
  - paired extremes for a whole (young and old, heaven and earth): render the whole,
    optionally keeping the pair as illustration;
  - repetition for intensity where the target does not intensify that way;
  - part for whole, whole for part, and an attribute standing for its owner: keep only if the
    reader recovers the referent.
- **A title or slogan with two senses.** If the target cannot carry both, decide whether the
  second is load-bearing, choose the reading the situation demands, and state what was lost.
- **A name that is also a word.** Check whether a proper name is being punned on, shortened or
  used as an attribute before translating; do not read it as the common word.

## 6. Culture-bound items

- **Do not swap in a target-culture item by default.** Use a general term, a short description
  or the borrowed word. Substitution is for a figurative point only, never for a report of
  what happened, never where it falsifies a claim the text makes, and only when the brief
  allows departing from the content. Record each one.
- **In a text the reader will act on, keep the thing's own name** beside any explanation (a
  dish, a form, an office, a street sign). The explanation is not what the reader can say or
  look for.
- **Glossing is asymmetric.** Add the minimum a target reader needs for a foreign reference,
  after checking that context does not already do it. Remove source explanations of things
  the target readers know better than the author did (their own country).
- **Test the explanation against what the reader knows.** An explanation that plays on the
  literal meaning of the source name says nothing to someone who does not know its parts.
- **Associations can invert.** A detail that evokes charm or prestige for source readers
  (old streets, a regional product, a foreign word) may evoke the opposite elsewhere. Identify
  the value the reader must share; where they do not share it, replace or neutralise the
  trigger instead of preserving it literally.
- **Foreign words used for effect stay foreign.** If the source switches language for
  prestige, modernity, rebellion or belonging, do not translate the switch away; if the target
  audience would no longer perceive it, mark the effect another way.
- **Is the thing a recognised category for the target reader?** If not, generalise or omit
  rather than explaining at length something that is no concern of theirs.
- **Brand names used as everyday words** become the generic term, unless the source really
  refers to the branded product.
- **Loans and calques.** Reject a loan when the target already has a word, when the loan is
  recent and easily replaced, or when a calque distorts target grammar or meaning. Keep
  established ones.

## 7. Variation and consistency

Ordinary vocabulary and terms follow opposite rules. For terms see `names-terms-quotations.md`.
For ordinary words, do not eliminate synonyms for a recurring idea: vary as original writing
in the target does, and harmonise only where a reader could reasonably think two different
things are meant. Tolerance for repetition differs by language (German and Italian prose
avoid it; English keeps functional repetition), so match the target's norm without
out-varying native writers.

## 8. Residue checks

Run these on the finished text.

- **Untranslated material.** Sweep for anything left in the source language (institution
  names, titles of works, quoted phrases) and require a reason for each. Source stems dressed
  in target spelling or endings count as untranslated.
- **Habitual pairings.** Your routine equivalences (X always becomes Y) are produced without
  deliberation; check that each fits this context.
- **Gender invented by a qualifier.** Do not disambiguate a gender-neutral noun by adding
  "male", "female" or "woman" to it. If the source does not mark gender, use the target's
  neutral term or let agreement carry what is actually known.
- **Borrowed solemnity.** Scan for archaic or scriptural register the source does not have.
  It is contamination from training text, common into languages with little other data.
- **English showing through.** When the target is a lower-resource or morphologically rich
  language, check specifically for English-shaped collocations, idioms and metaphor structure,
  not only for grammar. This applies even when English is neither source nor target.
